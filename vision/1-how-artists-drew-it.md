# How visual artists have pictured *The Pilgrim's Progress* (1678 to now), and what the game can borrow

Research note for the story game. Nothing here is to be pasted into the game; these are compositions, motifs and ways of staging to study and redraw in the game's own layered language.

**How this was gathered, and how far to trust it.** Web search worked; fetching pages mostly did not (this session's network allows only a few hosts, so `victorianweb.org`, `tfaoi.org`, `bunyanmeeting.co.uk`, `westminster-abbey.org`, `thecrankiefactory.com`, `en.wikipedia.org` and `archive.org` could not be opened; they would have to be allowed to go deeper). Wikimedia Commons' API was reachable and its category listings are quoted below exactly. So each item is marked:

- **[V]** verified today from a search result or a Commons listing (URL given);
- **[M]** from my own knowledge of the standard literature, not re-checked today. Treat [M] details (exact plate counts, plate titles, descriptions of single pictures) as leads to confirm before quoting them to the owner.

I could not look at the pictures themselves (Commons throttled image downloads), so descriptions of compositions are [M] unless a source describes them.

## Part 1. The artists, in order of time

### 1. The first editions: the "sleeping portrait" and the woodcuts (1679 to about 1700)

- The first edition (1678) had no pictures. The **third edition (1679)** added the frontispiece known as the **"sleeping portrait"**, engraved by Robert White (lettered "R.W. f." in the 1680 fourth edition) [V: https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00838501v1/file/White.pdf ; https://catalog.folger.edu/record/236096]. Bunyan sleeps in the foreground, head on hand, above a den with a lion in it; behind and above him his dream is drawn small: Christian, book in hand and burden on back, walking up a winding path from the City of Destruction (bottom) to the Celestial City (top, in rays) [M]. Commons: `File:The Pilgrim's Progress frontispiece and title page third edition 1679.jpg`, `File:Frontis., John Bunyan, head and shoulders portrait, sleeping - in background is his dream of the "Pilgrim's Progress" LCCN2006679915.jpg`, `File:John Bunyan dreaming.jpg` [V, Commons category "Pilgrim's Progress"].
- **Woodcuts** first appeared with the **fifth edition (1680)** [V: note in the 1875 facsimile, https://www.archive.org/download/pilgrimsprogress1875buny/pilgrimsprogress1875buny_bw.pdf]. The publisher also sold a set of copper "cuts" separately; copies vary (the Folger notes one with 13 plates where 11 are called for) [V]. The set that settled in the 1680s and 1690s is about fourteen small cuts, each with **a four-line verse beneath it** [M]. The usual subjects [M]: Christian meets Evangelist (reading, pointing); Worldly Wiseman and the hill; Good-will at the gate; the burden falling at the cross; the lions and the porter; Christian armed leaving the Palace; the fight with Apollyon; the Valley of the Shadow; Faithful burned, with the chariot waiting above; Giant Despair before Doubting Castle; the shepherds; the pilgrims riding the clouds / crossing the river.
- **How they work** [M]: crude, flat, side-on, no perspective; two or three moments shown in one picture (Christian appears twice); the path always visible as a line running through the cut; the Celestial City or the sun in a top corner of almost every cut, however far away the story still is. Frightening things are small and emblematic: Apollyon is a little winged, scaled devil no bigger than Christian; Faithful's fire is a neat bundle with a horse-drawn chariot hovering above it. Audience: ordinary households, children included.
- **The Dutch plates by Jan Luyken** (*Eens christens reyse na de eeuwigheyt*, Boekholt, Amsterdam, plates dated 1684 to 1687) are finer etchings of the same moments; the Rijksmuseum has them online with titles such as "Pilgrim knocks at a gate", "Christian climbs the hill Difficulty", "Christian fears being crushed by a falling rock", "Christian watches a man and a woman cleaning the floor", "Christian meets Apollyon", "Christian led through the river" [V: https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Christen-vreest-door-een-vallend-rotsblok-verpletterd-te-worden--c723d4af1a9b20cba1b78842a906b99c and neighbours]. Public domain; the Rijksmuseum releases its images freely.
- Later 18th-century editions: John Sturt's copper plates (1728) [M], endlessly recut chapbook versions (Commons has `Category:The Pilgrim's Progress (1840 chapbook)`) [V].
- **Worth borrowing:** the verse under the picture (the game's thin strip of words under the feet is the same idea); the city/light in the corner of every picture (the game's far light); "Christian twice in one picture" as licence for the game's wide establishing shots; the dreamer framing the dream.

### 2. The road maps (about 1775 to 1850)

- Engraved fold-out **"A Plan of the Road from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City"** sheets, the whole book as one winding road with every place drawn as a tiny landmark. Commons: `File:A Plan of the Road from the City of Destruction to the Coelestial City adapted to the Pilgrim's Progress (BM 1975,U.1140).jpg`, `File:John Bunyan, The Road From the City of Destruction to the Celestial City 1821 Cornell CUL PJM 1038 01.jpg`, `File:Pilgrim's Progress map large.JPG` [V]. Lancaster University's "Chronotopic Cartographies" project has modern analyses and a strip map [V: https://lancaster.ac.uk/chronotopic-cartographies/visualisations/pilgrims-progress/strip-map].
- **Worth borrowing:** these are the clearest precedent for the game's contents page. A plan of the road drawn like an engraving of the 1670s, each finished level inked in, would sit naturally in "THE CONTENTS." of the book. They also show which landmarks 350 years of readers treat as the essential ones: the Slough, the gate, the cross, the hill, the lions, the palace, two valleys, the fair, the castle, the mountains, the river.

### 3. Thomas Stothard and John Flaxman (1788 to 1790s)

- **Stothard:** sixteen designs engraved in stipple by Joseph Strutt, published 1788 to 1793 [V: Morgan Library and Yale Center for British Art records via https://themorgan.org/print/870476 , https://collections.britishart.yale.edu/catalog/tms:49729]; often called the first "fine art" illustrations of the book. Graceful neoclassical figures in soft oval compositions; Christian is a handsome youth; the tone is tender and sentimental, the terrors are softened (Apollyon is nearly a classical fallen angel) [M]. Audience: polite households. Public domain.
- **Flaxman:** pure outline drawings of about 1792; Commons has `File:John Flaxman - Illustration to Pilgrim's Progress - Google Art Project.jpg` [V]. The whole "outline style" of the next century (Selous, Bennett, Shields) descends from him and from the German Retzsch.
- **Worth borrowing:** Stothard's habit of making the *meeting* the picture (two figures and the gesture between them, almost no setting) suits the game's talk scenes; Flaxman's proof that a single clean contour carries a figure, which is the game's silhouette rule.

### 4. William Blake (about 1824 to 1827; unfinished)

- 28 watercolours (a 29th sheet is associated with the set), datable about 1824 to 1827, left unfinished at his death, some possibly coloured further by Catherine Blake; in the Frick Collection, New York (shown 1981 and again later; offered at Sotheby's in 1996) [V: https://bq.blakearchive.org/30.4.essick ; https://bq.blakearchive.org/6.3.wills ; https://frick.org/exhibitions/past/1981 ; Britannica's list of Blake's series]. The standard study is Gerda Norvig, *Dark Figures in the Desired Country* (Univ. of California Press) [V].
- **The scenes** (titles as usually given; the starred ones are confirmed by Commons file names) [M, * = V]: *John Bunyan dreams a dream; *Christian reading in his book; Christian meets Evangelist; Christian pursued by Obstinate and Pliable; Christian in the Slough of Despond; Christian drawn out by Help; Christian directed by Mr Worldly-Wiseman; *Christian fears the fire from the mountain; Christian falls at Evangelist's feet; Christian knocks at the wicket-gate; the gate opened by Good-will; *the man who dreamed of the Day of Judgment; *the man in the iron cage; *the man sweeping the Interpreter's parlour; Christian before the cross; Christian met by the three Shining Ones; Christian climbs the Hill Difficulty; *Christian at the arbour; Christian passes the lions; *Christian goes forth armed / with the shield of faith; *Christian fights Apollyon; Faithful's narrative; Vanity Fair; Faithful's martyrdom; Christian and Hopeful in Doubting Castle; their escape from Giant Despair; the shepherds of the Delectable Mountains; Christian and Hopeful in the river; *at the gates of heaven.
- Note the weighting: **about half of Blake's set covers the game's Levels 1 to 5.** He, like the game, thought the opening third of the book the most pictorial.
- **How he pictures it** [M]: the dreamer lies along the bottom of the first sheet and the whole dream arches over him like a rainbow of tiny figures. Christian is a bent, almost crushed figure; the burden is a huge dark globe strapped to him, as big as his torso, sometimes read as a sack of his own darkness. Light watercolour washes in rainbow tints; evil is drawn in the same pale, clear colours as good, only its *shapes* are cramped and heavy. The fire of Sinai is drawn as flames pouring down out of the rock over his head. Apollyon is vast, scaled, bat-winged, straddling the whole breadth of the way with Christian tiny beneath. The last sheet is all radiance: figures rising into a sun.
- A separate print, **"The Man Sweeping the Interpreter's Parlour"** (white-line metalcut, 1790s, reworked about 1822): the dust rises as a swarm of tiny demon faces, the sweeper is a bat-winged old man, the damsel a figure of light with her bowl [M].
- Commons: `Category:William Blake's Pilgrim's Progress` (11 files, e.g. `File:William Blake - John Bunyan Plate 8 Christian Fears the Fire from the Mountain.jpg`, `...Plate 20 The Christian Fights Apollyon.jpg`, `...Plate 28 At the Gates of Heaven.jpg`, `...Plate 1 Dreamen Dreams a Dream.jpg`) [V]. Public domain.
- **Worth borrowing:** the burden as a *shape of darkness* rather than luggage; the dream arching over the sleeper; flame drawn as falling water; dust drawn as something almost alive; the final picture as pure light with dark figures entering it.

### 5. John Martin, Turner, Samuel Palmer and the Romantics (1830 to 1850)

- **John Martin** made designs for **Southey's edition (John Murray and John Major, 1830)**: *The Valley of the Shadow of Death*, engraved by W. R. Smith, dated 1 February 1830 [V: https://royalacademy.org.uk/art-artists/work-of-art/the-valley-of-the-shadow-of-death], and *The Celestial City* [M; the Courtauld holds a Martin print of that title, https://gallerycollections.courtauld.ac.uk/object-g-1990-wl-5068-13]. The same edition has small wood engravings after William Harvey. Martin's Valley: a tiny armed figure on a ledge of path between a black abyss and towering crags, a cold light far ahead; his City: terraces of white architecture receding without end across a river, seen from a dark flowered foreground [M]. His later *Plains of Heaven* and *Celestial City and the River of Bliss* (1841, after Milton, not Bunyan [V: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Celestial_City_and_the_River_of_Bliss]) are the same vision at full size. The American panorama's Beulah scene was noted as close to Martin's Celestial City engraving [V, see section 7].
- **Turner** is said to have made a small vignette for Fisher's illustrated edition of 1836 [M, not confirmed today; check before quoting]. That edition's steel engravings after Henry Melville and others are on Commons: `File:The Land Of Beulah by H. Melville - Medium Quality.jpg`, `File:The Delectable Mountains by H. Melville...`, `File:The River Of The Water Of Life by H. Melville...`, `File:Christian Passing The Lions by A.B. Walter...`, `File:Pilgrim Setting Out On The Journey by A.B. Walter...` [V]. They are Turner-like: deep misty distances, tiny figures.
- **Samuel Palmer, *Christian Descending into the Valley of Humiliation*** (1848, watercolour and gouache, about 52 x 71 cm, Ashmolean, Oxford) [V: https://www.artrenewal.org/artworks/christian-descending-into-the-valley-of-humiliation/samuel-palmer/65254 ; Fitzwilliam record of the print after it]. A glowing dusk; the armed pilgrim small on a path going down a steep wooded hillside, the women of the Palace at the top watching him go; gold sky, plum and umber land [M]. The quietest and most beautiful picture of the moment before Apollyon.
- Also on Commons: `File:Harvey - john-bunyan-imagining-the-pilgrim-s-progress-in-bedford-gaol-1837.jpg`; `File:Joseph Noel Paton - Study of John Bunyan's 'Christian Arming'...` [V].
- **Worth borrowing:** Martin's rule that terror is scale plus one cold light (already the game's rule for Level 3); Palmer's descent at dusk for the opening of Level 8; Melville's Beulah for the last level's haze.

### 6. H. C. Selous, David Scott, Bennett, Shields, and the Victorian wood engravers (1844 to 1880)

- **Henry Courtney Selous**, outline designs for the Art-Union of London, 1844 (lithographs; Courtauld) [V: https://gallerycollections.courtauld.ac.uk/object-g-1990-wl-7891], in the Flaxman/Retzsch manner; later, with Paolo Priolo, the heavy wood engravings of Cassell's family edition (1860s) that fixed the popular Apollyon (armoured Christian on one knee, sword up, under a great scaled, winged, dart-throwing fiend) [M]. A Selous design, "They behold the fate of the apostate", was reused in the American panorama [V: https://victorianweb.org/art/illustration/selous/13.html].
- **David Scott**, forty designs etched by his brother William Bell Scott, published by Fullarton (1850/51) [V: Spectator, 1 March 1851, https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/1st-march-1851/19/fine-arts]. The reviewer praised the skies and the title vignette. Scott's manner is visionary and severe, close to Blake and Fuseli: large, muscular, half-classical figures in bare, dream-like spaces; he does not soften the dark scenes; made for adults [M]. Victorian Web's catalogue lists titles such as "Obstinate tries to persuade Christian and Pliable to return", "Christian is welcomed by Discretion and her sisters", "The fight with Apollyon", "They visit the spot where Christian fought Apollyon" (Part Two: a monument on the battlefield) [V: https://victorianweb.org/art/illustration/scott/index.html].
- **Charles H. Bennett** (Longman, 1860; about 118 to 120 drawings, preface by Charles Kingsley) [V: https://victorianweb.org/art/illustration/bennett/kingsley.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_H._Bennett_(illustrator)]. Almost no scenery: a gallery of **character heads** in outline, each person's nature read in the face, after Lavater's physiognomy; Kingsley advised "spiritual portraiture". Commons: `File:Pilgrims Progress Bennett-0008-0.jpg`, `...0009-0.png` [V].
- **Frederic Shields** (1864), strongly shaped by Retzsch; his crowded "Vanity Fair" and "Christian cast down by Apollyon" are the best known [V: https://victorianweb.org/victorian/art/illustration/shields/cooke.html ; .../shields/3.html].
- **Frederick Barnard and others** (Strahan, 1880; "one hundred illustrations by Frederick Barnard and others, engraved by Dalziel Brothers"; the others include William Small, J. D. Linton, E. G. Dalziel, Joseph Wolf) [V: https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/100218859 ; Spectator 27 Dec 1879]. Barnard, a Dickens illustrator, drew each character as a **full-length standing portrait** in 17th-century dress. Commons holds a long run of scans named `PProg NN ...` that appears to come from an edition using these designs (check the file pages): among them `PProg 04 p24 ISawAManClothedWithRags.jpg`, `09 Obstinate`, `10 Pliable`, `12 MrWorldlyWiseman`, `13 TheOtherGameHimAPull` (Good-will's pull), `15 ThereSatAManInAnIronCage`, `17 HisBurdenFellOffHisBack`, `18 BeholdThreeShiningOnes`, `19 Hypocrisy`, `20 Formalist`, `21 RunningGoingClambering` (the Hill), `23 HeAtLastFellIntoASlumber`, `24 Mistrust`, `25 Timorous`, `26 TheLionsWereChained`, `27 WatchfulThePorter`, `31 ACompanyOfFiends`, `32 OneOfTheWickedOnes` (the whisperer in the Valley), `33 GrinningAtPilgrims` (the old giant in the cave), `37 AManWhoseNameIsTalkative`, `38 VanityFair`, `39 LordHateGood`, `40-42 Envy, Superstition, Pickthank`, `43 TheJury`, `44 TheyBurnedHimAtTheStake`, `45 OneWhoseNameWasHopeful`, `46 LowConge` (By-ends bowing), `48 VainConfidence`, `49 GiantDespair`, `50 SadAndDoleful`, `51 Ignorance`, `52 AManWhomSevenDevilsHadBound`, `54 AtheistFellIntoAVeryGreatLaughter`, `57 OhISeeHimAgain` (in the river), `58 ThusTheyGotOver`, `59 KingsTrumpeter`, `60 ThenTheyTookHim` (Ignorance) [V, Commons listing]. Public domain. **This run is the single most useful free reference set for the game's later levels: one clear picture for almost every person the game still has to design.**
- **Worth borrowing:** from Bennett and Barnard, that each named person is one *posture* (the game's faces are plain, so the posture and outline must do what Bennett's faces do: By-ends is a low bow, Talkative an open hand and a tilted head, Timorous is running the wrong way looking back, Ignorance a jaunty stride); from Scott, the Part Two idea of the *monument* on Apollyon's field.

### 7. The American painters and the Moving Panorama (1840s to 1851)

- **Daniel Huntington**, *Mercy's Dream* (1841, later versions) and *Christiana and Her Children* (Part Two subjects): soft, Italianate, angel-and-maiden pictures that made Bunyan respectable for American painting [M]. **Frederic Church**, *Christian on the Borders of the Valley of the Shadow of Death* (1847): a small figure at the mouth of a cavernous dark valley, a supernatural light beyond [M]. **Jasper Cropsey** and Thomas Cole's circle painted pilgrim-and-far-city landscapes in the same years.
- **The Grand Moving Panorama of Pilgrim's Progress** [V: https://tfaoi.org/aa/3aa/3aa70.htm (Hardiman's history); https://tfaoi.org/newsm1/n1m487.htm ; https://livecinema.wikidot.com/the-grand-moving-panorama-of-pilgrim-s-progress ; https://hyperallergic.com/researchers-unroll-a-rare-19th-century-painted-panorama ; Library of Congress broadside "The story in color!! Bunyan's Pilgrim's progress. Illustrated by Messrs. Kyle, May, Huntington, Cropsey, Church, Darley, Dallas, and Paul Duggan", https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbpe.12204100/?st=text]:
  - conceived 1848 by Joseph Kyle and Edward Harrison May; the surviving second version painted 1850-51 by Kyle and Jacob Dallas; **distemper on muslin, about 8 feet high and 800 to 900 feet long**, wound between rollers;
  - designs supplied by National Academy painters: Church gave a variant of his Valley of the Shadow of Death; the **Land of Beulah** is attributed to Cropsey and was called "the landscape gem of the entire work" (and is close to John Martin); Huntington allowed copies of *Mercy's Dream* and *Christiana and Her Children*; one scene follows Selous; F. O. C. Darley and Paul Duggan are named on the broadside;
  - a showing lasted **about two hours, with music and a lecturer** reading and explaining as the canvas rolled;
  - lost for a century, found in 1996 in the store of the York Institute (now the **Saco Museum**, Maine); shown at the Montclair Art Museum in 1999 and conserved and shown again in Saco in 2012; book: Routhier, Avery and Hardiman, *The Painters' Panorama* (2015). (The task brief says Montclair; the owner is Saco, Montclair was the 1999 exhibition.) Photographs of the whole roll: https://archive.org/details/bunyanpanorama_combinedlarge ; project site https://panoramaofpilgrimsprogress.wordpress.com/history/ .
  - Route as a newspaper described it: Slough of Despond, Valley of the Shadow, Vanity Fair, the cave of Giant Despair, Beulah, up to the Celestial City.
- **Why it matters most of all:** it is the game, 175 years early: a side-scrolling painted roll of the book, each great scene designed after a master, joined by stretches of travelling landscape, with a storyteller and music. Things to study in the archive.org photographs [M for what follows]: how one scene's landscape is made to *run on* into the next (a hill's flank becomes the next valley's wall) so there are no cuts; how big set-pieces alternate with plain walking country; how the figures are kept large and near the bottom edge while the scenery towers; how the palette moves from cold and dark to gold at Beulah.

### 8. William Strang (etched 1885, published 1895)

- Fourteen etchings, published by John C. Nimmo in 1895 as a book and a portfolio; most etched in 1885 [V: https://data.fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/id/object/150332 ; https://mfaboston6.emuseum.com/objects/258066/the-pilgrims-progress]. Plates include "The Valley of the Shadow of Death / Christian and the Damned", "The Scourging of Faithful", "The Butcher and the Sheep" (an emblem from the Interpreter's house in Part Two) [V: National Galleries of Scotland; Cornell; Fitzwilliam]. Wikipedia: they show "austere sympathy with Bunyan's teaching" [V].
- Manner [M]: a pupil of Legros; rough, grave, peasant figures like Bunyan's own Bedfordshire neighbours; heavy shadow, little prettiness; a 17th-century plainness that suits the book better than any Victorian sweetness. For adults. Public domain.
- **Worth borrowing:** Christian as a working man, weathered and plain (the game's Help is already drawn this way); darkness made by massed tone rather than monsters.

### 9. The Rhead brothers (1898), Byam Shaw (1904), Copping (1903), the Brocks, Heath Robinson

- **George Woolliscroft, Frederick and Louis Rhead**, Century Co., New York, 1898 [V: https://openlibrary.org/borrow/ia/pilgrimsprogress1898buny ; poster at the Hood Museum and on Commons, `File:The New Illustrated Edition of Pilgrim's Progress ... Rhead ...tif`]. Single plates online at Old Book Illustrations: "The Mouth of Hell", "Beware of the Enchanted Ground", "He Deserveth to Die the Death", "Felt His Limbs to Fail", "Rejoice Not against Me" [V: https://www.oldbookillustrations.com/illustrations/mouth-hell/ etc.]. Manner [M]: Arts and Crafts pen work after Dürer and William Morris: thick black contours, flat pattern, decorative borders, **the caption lettered inside the picture on a scroll or tablet**. Strong, not gentle, but stylised enough to be safe.
- **Byam Shaw**, T. C. and E. C. Jack, 1904 (colour plates), and Mary Macgregor's retelling for the "Told to the Children" series (about 1906-7), e.g. "Do you see yonder wicket-gate?" [V: Spectator 19 Nov 1904; https://www.bridgemanimages.com/de/shaw/do-you-see-yonder-wicket-gate-.../asset/308570]. Commons: `File:The Pilgrim's Progress by Byam Shaw01.jpg` to `09` [V]. Late Pre-Raphaelite: jewel colour, strong outline, medieval dress, figures filling the frame [M]. The children's retelling is the first colour picture-book Bunyan.
- **Harold Copping**, 31 watercolours "in Puritan costume", Revell 1903 and the Religious Tract Society soon after [V: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Copping ; https://www.archive.org/download/pilgrimsprogress10buny/pilgrimsprogress10buny_bw.pdf]. He painted from costumed models (family and neighbours) for realism; the pictures were also sold as **missionary lantern slides** [V: https://lucerna.exeter.ac.uk/set/index.php?id=3004440]. Commons: `File:Illustratie van Harold Copping Pelgrimsreis.jpg`, `...2.jpg`; a full scan is `File:The pilgrim's progress ... (IA pilgrimsprogress10buny).pdf` [V]. Manner [M]: gentle, naturalistic, warm; a kindly bearded Evangelist; fear kept at a distance. For three generations this was the Sunday-school Christian.
- **R. H. Brock**, Nelson 1924: fifteen plates on Commons (`File:Illustration by RH Brock ... Page 013.jpg` etc.) [V]; public domain in the US, check elsewhere (he died 1943, so free in life+70 countries too).
- **W. Heath Robinson** illustrated an early edition (Sands, 1897), serious pen drawings from before his comic period [M, not confirmed today].
- **Jessie M. King:** I found no evidence today that she illustrated the book [V: search returned nothing]; treat the claim as doubtful.
- **Gustave Doré did not illustrate *The Pilgrim's Progress*** [M]. Pictures captioned as "Doré's Pilgrim's Progress" online are from his Bible, Milton or Dante. Today's searches turned up no Doré edition either.

### 10. The twentieth century

- **Robert Lawson** (Stokes, 1939), for Mary Godolphin's retelling in words of one syllable [V: https://treasuryofgreatchildrensbooks.com/browse/books/800]. Robust, humorous pen drawings by the *Ferdinand* illustrator; giants and fiends are grotesque rather than terrible [M]. Still in copyright.
- **Edward Ardizzone** (Faber, 1947) [V: https://royalacademy.org.uk/art-artists/book/the-pilgrims-progress-from-this-world-to-that-which-is-to-come]. Small cross-hatched pen vignettes set into the text; humane, a little comic, entirely English and homely; even Giant Despair looks like a bad-tempered farmer [M]. In copyright. The closest in *spirit* to a gentle version for children that keeps Bunyan's own words.
- **The Limited Editions Club (1941)** and later presses reproduced Blake's watercolours [M]. An **Eric Fraser / Folio Society** edition could not be confirmed today; treat as unverified.
- **Missionary editions**: the book was re-pictured in local dress in Chinese, Japanese and Korean editions of the 19th century; Commons has a Korean woodblock (`File:천로역정 목판.jpg`) and Khasi (1878) and Russian plates [V, Commons listing]. A precedent for the owner's "timeless clothes" rule: the story survives any costume.

### 11. Modern picture books and screen versions

- ***Dangerous Journey*** (text arranged by Oliver Hunkin from Bunyan's words; pictures by **Alan Parry**; Yorkshire Television, 1985, nine short parts narrated over the still pictures; recommended for ages 8 and up) [V: https://christianhistoryinstitute.org/store/dvd/500777d/dangerous-journey ; https://videolibrarian.com/reviews/tv/dangerous-journey/]. Dense pen-and-watercolour in the Rackham line: gnarled, earthy, crowded with detail; the monsters are drawn at full strength (a huge scaled Apollyon, hobgoblins in the Valley, a hulking Giant Despair) and many adults remember being frightened by it [M]. It is the reference for "Bunyan's words plus still pictures plus a narrator for children", but its busyness and its horrors are the opposite of the owner's taste. In copyright.
- ***Little Pilgrim's Progress*** (Helen Taylor's 1947 retelling with a child hero; the 2021 illustrated edition by Joe Sutphin) [V: https://www.barnesandnoble.com/w/little-pilgrims-progress-helen-taylor/1143955931]. Sutphin recasts everyone as **woodland animals** in soft pencil and sepia; the fearful things become approachable because they are animals [M]. In copyright.
- **Gary Schmidt and Barry Moser** (Eerdmans, 1994): Moser's transparent watercolours are mostly **portraits against plain or dark grounds**, lit from one side, the cast of many races and in a mix of period and modern dress [M; not confirmed today]. In copyright. Closest modern cousin of the game's "one light in a great dark".
- The 2019 animated film and various comic-book versions exist; none seems to offer anything the older artists do not.

### 12. Stained glass, bronze, and the museum

- **Bunyan Meeting, Bedford.** Bronze doors by **Frederick Thrupp**, ten relief panels of scenes from the book, modelled on Ghiberti's Gates of Paradise, shown at the Royal Academy in 1868, given by the Duke of Bedford in 1876 [V: https://www.artuk.org/discover/artworks/pair-of-bronze-doors-259755 ; Historic England list entry 1129018]. (Not Thornycroft.) Commons: `File:BunyanChurchDoor.JPG`, `File:Bunyan Church Door.png`, and what look like the single panels with their captions: `AsHeReadHeWeptAndTrembled.JPG`, `SimpleSaidISeeNoDanger.JPG`, `TheyHarrnessedHimFromHeadToHoof.JPG`, `IsThyStrengthSoSmall.JPG`, `ThySinBeForgivenThee.JPG`, `LetUsKeepOnOurWay.JPG`, `ForThoTheyKilledTheeThouArtStillAlive.JPG`, `BeOfGoodCheerMyBrother.JPG`, `EmmanuelsLand.JPG` [V, listing; attribution of these files to the doors is my reading, check the file pages]. ## Part 2. Cross-cutting themes

**The burden.** (a) The woodcuts: a round bundle tied high between the shoulders, as big as his head, the cords crossed on his chest. (b) Blake: a great dark globe, nearly as large as his torso, a shape rather than a thing. (c) Barnard, Copping and the Victorians: a realistic pedlar's pack. (d) Glass and bronze: the bundle shown *in mid-air*, half-way between his back and the tomb, at the cross. All agree on three things the game already does: it is behind and above him so it bends him forward; it never changes shape; and when it goes, his whole outline changes (he stands upright, arms lifted). Idea: let the burden's tone be the darkest thing in every picture of Levels 1 to 4 (Blake), so that at the Cross the darkest tone in the game leaves the screen.

**The far light / the City.** From 1679 it sits in a top corner of nearly every picture, even where the text does not mention it. Blake and Comper put it at the top with the dreamer at the bottom. Martin and Cropsey make it horizontal: terraces fading into light across water. The panorama saved its only fully golden passage for Beulah. This supports the game's rule of keeping the light in every level and rationing the gold.

**The path.** The old cuts and all the maps draw the way as a visible pale ribbon, "straight as a rule", with the wrong ways curving off it. Martin makes it a ledge. Windows make it a vertical climb. The game's flat side view is the woodcuts' own view. Idea: wherever a wrong way leaves (Worldly Wiseman's turning, By-path stile, the Flatterer's fork), show the two ways as two ribbons of different tone, as the maps do.

**The change of clothes.** Every illustrated tradition marks it: rags before the Cross; after it a clean coat, often white or pale (Blake, Copping), and from the Palace onward armour (helmet, shield with a cross, sword) which most artists keep on him to the end, though Bunyan lets it drop from view. In silhouette terms: bent and ragged-edged, then upright and smooth-edged, then with a shield's disc and a sword's line. Three outlines for three thirds of the game.

**The roll and the book.** The roll is always in his hand or his breast after the Cross and is the thing he looks at when frightened; the book belongs to the first pictures only (the game already drops it after Level 1).

**The dreamer.** Almost every great version opens (and the glass closes) with Bunyan asleep: White, Blake, Comper, Harvey. The game's opened book on the title page plays this part; a sleeping figure is not needed, but the *frame* is traditional.

**Frightening things, by audience.** Small and emblematic (woodcuts, glass, Ardizzone, Lawson); softened into classical grace (Stothard, Huntington, Copping); made sublime by scale and light with almost no monster visible (Martin, Church, Palmer); drawn at full nightmare strength (Scott, Selous/Cassell, Shields, Parry). For ages 7 to 10 and this owner, the Martin/Church/Palmer way is the proven one: **the place is frightening, the creature is hardly seen.** For Faithful's death the tradition for children is unanimous: show the **chariot and horses coming for him through the clouds**, not the fire (woodcuts, Thrupp's panel caption "For though they killed thee, thou art still alive", Kempe's window).

## Part 3. Level by level: ideas to borrow

Each idea is tagged with its source. All are meant to be redrawn in the game's own terms (one tone a layer, flat side view, few things, one light).

### Level 1. City of Destruction (built)
- The far light and tiny city in the upper corner from the very first picture (1679 frontispiece and every woodcut).
- "As he read, he wept and trembled": the first Thrupp door panel is exactly the game's opening pose; glass and bronze both make this one figure, book open, head bowed, family in a doorway behind (Thrupp; Blake's "Christian reading in his book").
- Evangelist's pointing arm as the single strong diagonal of the picture, the roll hanging from his other hand (woodcuts; Elstow window; Byam Shaw's "Do you see yonder wicket-gate?").
- Fingers in ears, running, with wife and children reaching from the door: Blake and Barnard both make the *reaching arms* the picture. The game has this.
- Obstinate and Pliable as two postures: one planted, arms folded or pointing home; one leaning half-forward, half-back (Bennett's heads; Barnard's `PProg 09/10`; David Scott).
- A contents page drawn as the 18th-century "Plan of the Road" (road maps).

### Level 2. Slough of Despond (built)
- Blake gives it two pictures: sinking, and Help's hand. Help always reaches *down from firm ground on the far side*; the two arms make one line. The game has this.
- Pliable climbing out *on the side nearest his own house*, seen from behind, is the comic beat in Barnard and Copping.
- The steps drawn as a faint dotted line across the mire in the old maps: a nice confirmation of the game's lights on the stones.
- The panorama opened its dark passages here: a wide, low, empty marsh under a heavy sky before any figure is seen (Moving Panorama).

### Level 3. Mr Worldly Wiseman and the hill (built)
- Blake's "Christian fears the fire from the mountain": flame *pouring downward* off the overhang like water, Christian shrinking beneath it. A possible touch for the wide shot: one thin fall of fire from the lip of the overhang.
- Luyken's plate is titled "Christian fears being crushed by a falling rock": the overhang, not the fire, is the subject, as the game's line says.
- Wiseman as a prosperous silhouette: broad hat, cane, cloak, belly forward (Barnard `PProg 12`; Copping). The game has this.
- Evangelist raising him by the hand is a favourite relief subject (Bedford statue plinth).

### Level 4. The Wicket-Gate and the Interpreter's House (house built; gate planned)
- **Good-will's pull**: Barnard's `PProg 13` ("The other gave him a pull") and Blake's "The gate opened by Good-will" both show the two figures joined by the arm across the threshold, dark outside, light inside. Luyken's "Pilgrim knocks at a gate". Comper's window pairs Christian with Good-will.
- The arrows from Beelzebub's castle: older cuts put a tiny castle on a hill in the far left with a thread of darts; small and far is the traditional answer to "shown or sound only".
- The verse over the gate cut on a tablet (Rheads' lettered scrolls): the game's carved stone is in this tradition.
- **The parlour**: Blake's print makes the dust a living cloud and the damsel a figure of light; Luyken shows a man and a woman cleaning. Idea: in the drawn parlour let the stirred dust briefly take faint, swirling, almost-creature shapes before the water lays it (very subtle, as the owner liked for the bog's fears).
- **The man in the iron cage**: Blake and Barnard (`PProg 15`) both draw him seated, hunched, hands hanging, the bars straight and regular against a soft figure. Regular bars against a soft shape is the whole picture.
- **The dream of judgment**: Blake draws the dreamer starting up in bed with the vision opening above him in a burst of light, the room otherwise dark. That is the game's room 7 already; Blake confirms putting the vision *above the bed* rather than on a wall.
- **The fire and the oil**: older cuts show the wall as a plain upright line dividing the picture in two halves, devil with bucket one side, Christ with a vessel the other, both visible at once to *us* while Christian sees only one. The game reveals the second half by walking round; a brief moment when both halves are seen together would quote the cut.
- Strang's "The Butcher and the Sheep" is a reminder that Part Two has more emblem rooms if a later version wants them.

### Level 5. The Cross and Sepulchre (built)
- The burden shown **in the air**, mid-tumble, between his back and the tomb's mouth (woodcuts; Barnard `PProg 17`; Kempe's Southwark window). One held frame of this is the most quoted image of the whole book.
- Christian's arms flung up and body suddenly straight; nearly every artist changes his spine here.
- The three Shining Ones as three tall verticals of light, arriving from the upper corner where the far light has been all along (Blake's "Christian met by the three Shining Ones"; Barnard `PProg 18`; Thrupp's panel "Thy sin be forgiven thee"; Bedford statue relief).
- The tomb as a plain dark mouth low in the picture, lower than his feet (woodcuts put it at the foot of the mound, the game has it in the hollow).
- Blake keeps the cross bare and small and lets the *light behind it* be the subject. The game has this after Friedrich.
- The new coat: pale or white in Blake and Copping; in silhouette, a smooth hem instead of a ragged one.

### Level 6. Simple, Sloth and Presumption; Formalist and Hypocrisy; Hill Difficulty; the arbour; the lions
- **Three sleepers in fetters** at the foot of the hill, lying in a row with irons on their heels (Thrupp panel "Simple said, I see no danger"): three low horizontal shapes beside the upright walker.
- **Formalist and Hypocrisy tumbling over the wall** on the left, legs first, where Christian came through the gate (Barnard `PProg 19/20`; Scott). Comic and harmless.
- **Three ways at the foot of the hill**: straight up, and two level roads curving off to left and right called Danger and Destruction, into a dark wood and a field of dark mountains. The maps draw this as a clear fork; a perfect "two ribbons" picture.
- **The climb**: Barnard's caption "running, going, clambering": the figure's pose changes three times up the slope, upright, bent, hands and knees. Blake's "Christian climbs the Hill Difficulty" and Luyken's plate make the hill a single steep diagonal filling the picture. After Friedrich, one great slope and a small figure from behind.
- **The spring at the bottom** where he drinks before climbing (a quiet touch act).
- **The arbour** half-way up: Blake's "Christian at the arbour" shows him asleep, the roll slipping from his hand. A small leafy bower as the one soft, green thing on a bare slope; the roll left as a tiny pale mark on the bench when he walks on (the child may notice it; he does not).
- **Timorous and Mistrust** running *down* past him, looking back (Barnard `PProg 24/25`): two figures moving against the scroll direction.
- **Going back for the roll**: the same slope walked down and up again at dusk; several artists set the lions at nightfall, the Palace's lit window beyond.
- **The lions**: chained, one each side of a narrow way, the chains hard to see in the dark (Barnard `PProg 26`; Blake; Melville/Walter's steel engraving `Christian Passing The Lions`). Tradition for children: lions sitting or lying like heraldic beasts, the porter Watchful small in a lit gateway beyond calling "keep in the midst of the path". Two dark masses, two glints of eye, a taut line of chain that shows only when the light from the lodge catches it.

### Level 7. Palace Beautiful
- A lit lodge and a great house on the hill's top at night, one warm doorway (Rembrandt, as planned); the porter with a lantern (Barnard `PProg 27` "Watchful the porter").
- **Welcome by the sisters** at the door: Scott's "Christian is welcomed by Discretion and her sisters"; a row of women in the doorway light, Christian below on the step. Glass loves this for its ring of pale figures.
- **Supper and talk**: Barnard `PProg 29` "Read to him some of the worthy acts": a table, a book, one candle.
- **The chamber called Peace**, "whose window opened toward the sun-rising": every illustrator who draws it puts a single east window with dawn in it and Christian waking. A one-picture room: dark chamber, the window a pane of the far light.
- **The armoury**: Thrupp's panel "They harnessed him from head to foot"; Paton's "Christian arming" (Commons). The women buckle on helmet, breastplate, shield, sword; relics on the wall (Moses' rod, David's sling, the jawbone, Gideon's trumpets and pitchers) as a row of small silhouettes the child can touch one by one.
- **The view from the roof**: the Delectable Mountains far to the south, "Immanuel's Land", seen from the leads on a clear morning (Thrupp caption "Emmanuel's Land"; Melville's engraving). A Friedrich picture: figures from behind at a parapet, layer upon layer of pale hills, the far light over them. This could be the level's one great picture.
- **The parting**: Blake's extra sheet, "Christian with the shield of faith taking leave of his companions"; then Palmer's descent.

### Level 8. Apollyon, in the Valley of Humiliation
- **Open with Palmer**: the long golden-dusk descent, the sisters small at the top giving him "a loaf of bread, a bottle of wine, and a cluster of raisins". Beauty first.
- The valley itself as a wide, low, quiet meadow (Part Two calls it the best ground in all those parts): the fight is frightening because the place is so plain.
- **Apollyon by Martin's rule, not Selous's**: seen first as a shape far off "coming over the field", then as a vast wing-shadow that covers the way from side to side ("straddled quite over the whole breadth of the way", Blake). Show mostly wings, smoke and the glow of the darts; the body one dark tone.
- The **darts** as thin streaks of fire "thick as hail" caught on the shield's disc (woodcuts; Thrupp's "Is thy strength so small?").
- The fall and the lost sword, then the sword regained: every version turns on the single upward thrust from one knee (Selous/Cassell; Shields' "Christian cast down"; Blake's "Christian beaten down by Apollyon"; Bedford statue relief; Comper). In silhouette: a kneeling figure, a line of sword, a huge shape recoiling upward.
- Apollyon "spread forth his dragon's wings and sped him away": he *leaves upward and shrinks*; the sky clears.
- **The hand with leaves of the tree of life** healing his wounds: a single hand of light from the picture's edge holding a sprig (woodcuts show it as a hand from a cloud). A lovely, gentle touch act, an echo of Help's and Evangelist's hands.
- Scott's Part Two picture of a **monument on the battlefield** suggests leaving a small stone or his dented shield's mark where it happened.

### Level 9. Valley of the Shadow of Death
- **Martin (1830) and Church (1847) are the models**: a narrow pale ledge of path, a deep ditch on the right, a quag on the left, crags closing overhead, one small cold light far ahead. Almost nothing else. Strang's plate masses the dark the same way.
- The two men running back at the entrance ("Back! back!"), again moving against the scroll.
- The **mouth of hell** "hard by the way-side": the Rheads' plate and the old cuts draw a cave mouth breathing flame and smoke with small shapes in it. For this game: a red-amber glow from below the path's edge, smoke crossing the way, sounds; no figures (Barnard's `PProg 31` "A company of fiends" shows what to leave out).
- **"All-prayer"**: he puts up his sword and prays; older cuts show him with hands lifted and the sword sheathed. A change of pose the child triggers.
- **The whisperer** who steps up behind him (Barnard `PProg 32`): a second shadow that briefly joins his own and falls away; no creature drawn.
- **The voice ahead** ("Though I walk through the valley...", Faithful's voice): a second tiny light ahead on the same ledge.
- **Daybreak**: Bunyan stops to look back at what he came through by morning light. Many artists skip this; it is the level's beauty: the same valley seen in grey dawn, the ditch and the quag now visible and harmless behind him.
- The second half, full of snares, pits and nets, drawn in the maps as tiny marks along the road: pale lines of net and dark holes that the candle-light of morning shows.
- **Pope and Pagan's cave**: Barnard's `PProg 33` "Grinning at pilgrims": an old giant sitting in the cave mouth, too stiff to come out, biting his nails, bones about. For children, Ardizzone's and Lawson's way: a slumped, toothless old shape that only mutters. Possibly sound and a seated silhouette only; needs the owner's ruling.

### Level 10. Faithful (and Talkative)
- Christian on **a little rise** seeing Faithful ahead and calling; he runs, overtakes, "smiles vain-gloriously", stumbles, and Faithful helps him up (the old cuts show the stumble). Another hand.
- The two walking side by side as one double silhouette: the book's first true company. Blake's "Faithful's narrative" shows them walking while Faithful's story floats as small figures above them: **thought-pictures over their heads**, exactly the device the owner liked in Level 2's shining city. Faithful's tales (Wanton, Adam the First, Moses who knocked him down, Shame) could each be one faint vignette in the sky as they walk, with no stopping.
- **Talkative**: "a tall man, and something more comely at a distance than at hand" (Barnard `PProg 37`). A handsome silhouette far off that looks slighter and more fidgety as he nears; always gesturing, walking a little apart.
- Evangelist's third coming, warning of the fair: his familiar outline and pointing arm return.

### Level 11. Vanity Fair
- The planned master (Japanese woodblock night scene) fits what glass and Shields do: **a wall of stalls, flags and lanterns** behind two plain grey figures walking straight through without looking (Shields; Barnard `PProg 38`; Blake's "Vanity Fair"; Comper).
- The contrast everyone uses: the crowd in gaudy motley, the pilgrims in plain clothes with their eyes up ("they looked upwards"). In the game's terms: the only level where the *background* has many colours and the heroes stay monochrome.
- "We buy the truth": a merchant's outstretched tray, their hands at their ears (an echo of Level 1's fingers in ears).
- **The cage**: the two in a cage in the market, mocked (old cuts; Barnard). Regular bars again, quoting room 6 of the Interpreter's House.
- **The trial**: Lord Hate-good high on a bench, the three witnesses Envy, Superstition and Pickthank as three postures, the jury as a row of twelve heads (Barnard `PProg 39` to `43`; Bennett's heads; the Rheads' "He deserveth to die the death"). A row of twelve dark heads along the bottom of the picture is a strong, simple, slightly comic image.
- **Faithful's death** for children: by universal tradition the **chariot and horses** "waiting for Faithful" behind the crowd, taking him "up through the clouds with sound of trumpet, the nearest way to the Celestial Gate" (woodcuts; Thrupp's panel; Kempe's window; Blake's "Faithful's martyrdom" gives most of the sheet to the ascent). The scourging and the stake (Strang's "Scourging of Faithful", Barnard `PProg 44`) are what to leave out. One could show only: the crowd closing round, a glow, then the chariot of light rising over the roofs toward the far light, and Christian looking up. Needs the owner's ruling.
- **Hopeful** stepping out of the fair's crowd to join him (Barnard `PProg 45`): one figure in the motley crowd turns grey and plain.

### Level 12. Hopeful, By-ends, Demas and the silver mine, Lot's wife
- **By-ends** as a low sweeping bow, hat in hand (Barnard `PProg 46` "Low congé"), and his three friends as a cluster of well-fed outlines falling behind.
- **The plain called Ease**, short and pleasant: a few seconds of open, sunny, easy walking (the maps make it a tiny patch).
- **The hill Lucre and Demas**: a "gentleman-like" figure standing a little off the road at a pit's edge, beckoning with one hand, the ground "deceitful" and breaking at the rim (old maps draw the mine as a dark hole in a small hill). By-ends' party go over to look and are not seen again: figures walking to the rim and simply sinking from view, no fall shown.
- **The pillar of salt**: "a woman transformed into the shape of a pillar", with writing on it, "Remember Lot's wife". A single pale standing shape by the road that from far off looks like a waiting woman and close up is stone; a touchable inscription like the gate's stone (the Rheads would letter it).
- **The River of the Water of Life** and its meadow of lilies with fruit trees on both banks, where they sleep safely: Melville's engraving (`The River Of The Water Of Life`). The level's beauty and its rest; water that truly mirrors.

### Level 13. By-path Meadow, Doubting Castle, Giant Despair, the key called Promise
- **The stile**: the rough stony way and, over a stile, a soft green path running alongside the fence. Maps and Copping make this the picture: two parallel ways, one fence. Christian goes first and persuades Hopeful: his fault, a moment of acting.
- **Vain-confidence** walking ahead in the dusk and vanishing into a pit (Barnard `PProg 48`): a figure ahead that is simply no longer there, then a sound.
- Night, thunder, rising water: they cannot get back; sleep in "a little shelter".
- **Giant Despair** by Ardizzone's and Lawson's rule, or by Martin's: either a grim, heavy, almost homely farmer with a crab-tree cudgel (Barnard `PProg 49`), or, better for this game, seen only as **legs and a shadow**: he is so tall the picture cannot hold him (the game's own rule for showing size: "what does not fit"). The castle a black block on the dawn sky (the panorama's "cave of Giant Despair"; old cuts show the giant at the castle door and the pilgrims tiny).
- **The dungeon**: Blake's "Christian and Hopeful in Doubting Castle" and Barnard's `PProg 50` "Sad and doleful": two figures sitting close in the dark, one slit of light. Hopeful comforting Christian reverses their roles. The beatings and the giant's counsel to kill themselves are what every children's version omits.
- The giant's **fits in sunshiny weather** ("he lost for a time the use of his hand"): light itself disarms him; a beam through the grating makes the shadow falter.
- **The key called Promise**, "in my bosom": Christian draws it from his coat, where the roll is. A small glint of the far light's own colour; three locks (dungeon door, castle-yard door, the iron gate that "went damnable hard"), three touch acts, each a note. Blake's "Escape from Giant Despair" has them running out into morning with the giant collapsing behind.
- **The pillar they set up** at the stile to warn others: the child's last act, a carved stone.

### Level 14. The Delectable Mountains
- Already seen from the Palace roof in Level 7: arriving there should feel like stepping into that far picture.
- **Four shepherds** leaning on their staves by the highway: Knowledge, Experience, Watchful, Sincere (Blake's "The shepherds of the Delectable Mountains"; old cuts; Melville). Four upright figures with crooks and a flock as a pale moving layer.
- Gardens, orchards, vineyards, fountains: the game's first truly gold-green level.
- **The hill called Error**: a sheer cliff seen from the top, with something far below. Tradition for children is to show only the pilgrims peering over and the shepherds holding them back.
- **Mount Caution**: from it, far off, small figures of blind men wandering among tombs (Barnard `PProg 52` area; Selous's "They behold the fate of the apostate" was a panorama scene): tiny, distant, and tied back to the stile of Level 13.
- **The door in the hill-side** (a by-way to hell): a door in a green bank with smoke under it; sound only.
- **The hill called Clear and the perspective glass**: "their hands did shake", so they see the gate only dimly. The obvious and loveliest act of the level: the child holds the glass, a round view opens on the far light, and it trembles, never quite sharp; "they thought they saw something like the gate, and also some of the glory of the place". Thrupp's "Emmanuel's Land" panel and many windows show the two looking through the glass with a shepherd steadying it.
- The shepherds' two gifts: a note of the way, and two warnings (beware the Flatterer; do not sleep on the Enchanted Ground), which set up the next two levels.

### Level 15. Ignorance, the Flatterer's net, Atheist
- **Ignorance** as "a very brisk lad" coming down "a little crooked lane" from the country of Conceit (Barnard `PProg 51`): a light, jaunty walk, always a little behind, never catching up.
- **The man bound with seven cords** carried back by seven devils to the door in the hill (Barnard `PProg 52`): for this game, at most a far-off glimpse; probably left out.
- **Little-faith's story**: could be thought-pictures above them as they walk (Blake's device): three dark shapes, a small man asleep, a purse, his jewels kept.
- **The fork and the Flatterer**: "a man black of flesh, but covered with a very light robe"; in silhouette, a figure whose pale robe is the only light thing on a dark body. He leads them round by degrees until their backs are to the City: the far light slowly slides from ahead to behind as they walk, without their noticing. A clean, quiet, visual way to tell it.
- **The net**: Copping's "Entangled in the net" [V: Bridgeman record]; old cuts show them under a mesh on the ground, the white robe fallen off the Flatterer. Fine pale lines dropping over two figures.
- **The Shining One with a whip of small cord** who cuts them free: show the freeing and the pointing back to the way; the chastising is traditionally softened or omitted for children.
- **Atheist** walking "softly and alone, all along the highway" toward them with his back to the City, and his "very great laughter" (Barnard `PProg 54`): one figure passing the other way, laughing, growing small behind them.

### Level 16. The Enchanted Ground
- The Rheads' "Beware of the Enchanted Ground" [V]. A drowsy air: the level where *the picture itself* tries to go to sleep: colours fading toward one warm grey, the scroll slowing, sounds softening, arbours with soft benches by the way (an echo of the arbour on Hill Difficulty where he lost the roll, which the child will remember).
- Hopeful wants to lie down; Christian keeps him talking: "to prevent drowsiness in this place, let us fall into good discourse". The traditional picture is two men walking arm in arm, one propping the other. The talk (Hopeful's story of how he came to faith) as thought-pictures again, or simply as the hymn's notes laid along the way to keep them awake: each note touched brightens the picture a little.
- Ignorance still following far behind, small.
- The level ends as the air clears into Beulah: the opposite of Level 2's mist coming down.

### Level 17. Beulah, the River, the Celestial City
- **Beulah**: Cropsey's panorama scene, "the landscape gem of the entire work", and Melville's `The Land Of Beulah`: orchards and vineyards on gentle slopes, the air full of birdsong, the City visible across the river, "builded of pearls and precious stones" and so bright with the sun on it that they fall sick with longing and cannot yet look at it "but through an instrument". Turner, as planned: forms dissolving in gold. The King's gardeners at the gates of gardens.
- Two **men in raiment that shone like gold** meet them and walk with them to the river.
- **The River**: no bridge, deep, "deeper or shallower as you believe". Blake's "Christian and Hopeful in the river" and Barnard's `PProg 57` "Oh, I see him again" make it two heads and lifted arms in dark water with light on the far bank; Thrupp's caption "Be of good cheer, my brother"; Luyken's "Christian led through the river". The game's Slough frames come back here (the notes already plan to reuse the wading videos): the first deep water since Level 2, but now Hopeful holds him up as Help once did, "I feel the bottom, and it is good". A deliberate rhyme with Level 2 that children will feel.
- The far bank: they come out **without their mortal garments**, light and quick; the hill of the City climbed "with ease" though it is higher than the clouds, the two Shining Ones leading them by the arms (Barnard `PProg 58` "Thus they got over"; the old cut of the pilgrims "riding the clouds").
- **The King's trumpeters** in white and shining raiment, and the bells (Barnard `PProg 59`; Comper puts the bells and the Lamb in the tracery). The hymn whole, and bells.
- **The gate**: writing over it in letters of gold; they give in their **certificates**: the roll from the Cross, carried in the tray for twelve levels, is handed in at last (the child's final act). Blake's "At the gates of heaven": dark small figures entering a radiance; Martin's terraces beyond.
- "I wished myself among them": the dreamer's last look through the gate before it shuts; the 1679 frontispiece and Comper's window suggest ending where the book began, on the book itself closing.
- **Ignorance** ferried over by Vain-hope and turned away (Barnard `PProg 60` "Then they took him") is Bunyan's actual last scene and is nearly always omitted for children. Needs the owner's ruling; the gentlest tradition simply does not show it.

## Part 4. Short list of free reference sets to study first

1. **The 1850-51 Moving Panorama**, photographed whole: https://archive.org/details/bunyanpanorama_combinedlarge (a painted side-scroller of the entire book).
2. **Commons `PProg NN` series** (Barnard and others): one figure study per character for Levels 6 to 17.
3. **Blake's watercolours**: Commons `Category:William Blake's Pilgrim's Progress` (burden as dark globe; thought-pictures over walking figures; the last radiance).
4. **Jan Luyken's etchings**, Rijksmuseum (free images): the 1680s view of each scene.
5. **Martin's *Valley of the Shadow of Death*** (Royal Academy record) and **Palmer's *Christian Descending into the Valley of Humiliation*** (Ashmolean): Levels 9 and 8.
6. **The road maps** on Commons: the contents page.
7. **Copping's 31 plates**: Commons PDF `The pilgrim's progress ... (IA pilgrimsprogress10buny).pdf` (the gentlest full set).
8. **Thrupp's door panels** on Commons (ten scenes chosen for a church door, with their captions).

All of these are before 1930 and in the public domain. Lawson, Ardizzone, Parry, Sutphin and Moser are in copyright: ideas only.
