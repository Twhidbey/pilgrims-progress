# 10. Music, researched wide: Bunyan's world, the hymn, the games, and a sound script for all seventeen levels

Researcher 10 of the art deep dive. Ideas only: nothing built, nothing spent, no game file touched. Written from what I know as of November 2026. **I could not listen to anything from here.** Every judgement of how a piece sounds comes from published description and its score, not from my ears. Points I am not sure of are marked **(unverified)**. Links point to stable pages (Wikipedia, hymnary.org, IMSLP, CPDL, Musopen, Wikimedia Commons). Where I give a YouTube or archive.org *search* link, that is so the owner can listen; it does not mean the recording is free to use.

**The settled rules this report keeps to (from `CLAUDE.md`, "Music" and Level 5):**
silence and the world's own sounds are the ground; music is punctuation at a few moments, never under the hymn's lights; Amazing Grace is found as lights one note at a time, and is heard whole only three times (Level 1's gate, the Cross, the Celestial Gate on the King's trumpets), with at most three remembered phrases in between; after the Cross there is a second walking tune (an old free tune); the choir sounds only where the Shining Ones or the City are (`vision/7-awe-cross-choir.md`); Lyria prompts name no composer or piece; the owner's ears decide every level.

## Part 1. The music of Bunyan's own world

### 1.1 The metrical psalm: the only song most of his hearers knew

In the English parish church of the 1600s, people did not sing hymns. They sang the Psalms put into rhyming metre, almost always in Common Metre (8.6.8.6, the metre of "Amazing Grace"). The book was **Sternhold and Hopkins**, the "Old Version" (*The Whole Booke of Psalmes*, John Day, 1562). It was bound into the back of Bibles for 150 years, so the Bible in Christian's hand would very likely have had psalm tunes at its back.
- Background: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sternhold_and_Hopkins
- **Old Hundredth** (Genevan Psalter, 1551, usually credited to Louis Bourgeois; in English with William Kethe's "All people that on earth do dwell", 1561). It is the oldest tune English congregations still sing. Long notes at the start and end of each line, plain steps, no leaps worth naming. https://hymnary.org/tune/old_hundredth_bourgeois
- **Tallis's Canon** (Thomas Tallis, for Archbishop Parker's psalter, *c.*1567). Its eight bars can be sung as a round, the second voice entering one bar behind the first. That makes it a ready-made "two companions walking" tune. https://hymnary.org/tune/tallis_canon_tallis
- **St Flavian** (Day's Psalter, 1562). A plain Common Metre tune. https://hymnary.org/tune/st_flavian
- **The Scottish Psalter of 1615 and 1635** gave the "common tunes" **York**, **Dundee** (called *French*), **Martyrs** and **Dunfermline**. York is the tune Bunyan's English contemporaries knew best of all. Isaac Watts later complained that half the parishes of England sang it. https://hymnary.org/tune/york_milton

**Lining out.** The Westminster Assembly's *Directory for Public Worship* (1644) told the clerk or precentor to read or sing each line before the congregation sang it, because many could not read. The result is a call and a slow, swelling answer: one voice, then many, the tune dragged out and decorated. Lining out lasted longest in the Gaelic psalm singing of the Hebrides and in some Old Regular Baptist churches of Kentucky. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lining_out

*What it gives the game.* There are two strong ideas here.
- **One voice ahead, then answered** is the very shape of the Shining Ones' choir (one treble, then many voices). It is also the shape of the Valley of the Shadow, where Christian "perceived the voice of a man as going before him, saying, Though I walk through the valley ..." (`{165}`, Faithful). A lone voice lining out a psalm in the dark, far ahead, is truer to Bunyan's world than any score.
- **Every psalm tune is Common Metre, like Amazing Grace.** A child cannot tell the forms apart, but the player hears one family of song from the first level to the last.

### 1.2 Bunyan and music: the bell-ringer who gave it up, and the Bedford quarrel

- **The bells of Elstow.** In *Grace Abounding* (1666, §33 to 34) Bunyan says he loved bell-ringing as a young man. His conscience then told him it was vain, so he gave it up, but he still went to watch. Then he would not even stand in the steeple door, for fear a bell or the steeple would fall on him. A bell standing for a heart half-turned is Bunyan's own image. Text: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/654 (Grace Abounding). The Elstow belfry, a detached tower, still stands. *Use:* the bells of the City at the end ("all the bells in the city rang again for joy", `{400}`) answer bells Bunyan himself once rang and gave up. Bells should be heard nowhere else in the game except, very faintly, Level 1's town.
- **The prison flute and the iron fiddle.** Bedford tradition says that in gaol Bunyan made a flute from a stool leg, and that the John Bunyan Museum keeps a metal violin with his name on it. **(Both unverified; treat as legend.)** https://www.bunyanmeeting.co.uk (museum)
- **The singing controversy.** Bunyan's Bedford congregation was an open-membership Independent and Baptist church. In the 1670s to 1690s English Baptists argued over whether a congregation should sing at all, or only in psalms, or hymns of human making. Benjamin Keach introduced a hymn after the Lord's Supper at Horselydown around 1673 and defended it in *The Breach Repaired* (1691); Isaac Marlow wrote against him. **(I do not know of a record of Bunyan's own church's practice; unverified.)** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Keach
- **What Bunyan himself wrote** is telling. *The Pilgrim's Progress* is full of singing: Christian sings at the Cross, at sunrise in the chamber Peace, after the Valley and after Doubting Castle. Part Two (1684) is fuller still. The Interpreter's household sings; at Palace Beautiful there is music. Mr Ready-to-halt dances on his crutches with Much-afraid, who plays the viol, and her father Mr Despondency is so glad he goes to the music. In the Valley of Humiliation a shepherd boy sings "He that is down needs fear no fall". **The book is on the side of song.** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/131 (Part One and Two)

### 1.3 Bunyan's own hymn: "Who would true valour see"

- It is Mr Valiant-for-Truth's song in Part Two (1684): "Who would true valour see, / Let him come hither ... There's no discouragement / Shall make him once relent / His first avowed intent / To be a pilgrim." It is the only song from the book that is still widely sung.
- Percy Dearmer softened it as **"He who would valiant be"** for *The English Hymnal* (1906), taking out the hobgoblins and the foul fiend. Ralph Vaughan Williams set it to **Monks Gate**, a Sussex folk tune he collected in 1904 from a Mrs Harriet Verrall of Monks Gate, near Horsham. https://hymnary.org/tune/monks_gate
- **St Dunstan's** (Charles Winfred Douglas, 1917) is the American tune, closer to Bunyan's own words. Douglas died in 1944, so its copyright status needs checking before use. https://hymnary.org/tune/st_dunstans_douglas
- **Copyright care.** The folk melody of Monks Gate, as sung, is traditional. Vaughan Williams' *harmonisation* is his: he died in 1958, so it is in copyright in the UK until the end of 2028 **(check; US status differs)**. **Use the bare tune, harmonised by us in code.**
- *Use:* this is the natural **second walking tune** after the Cross. It is Bunyan's own words, in 6.5.6.5.6.6.6.5 with a striding 3/4 lilt, and its last line is "To be a pilgrim". It belongs to Part Two in the book, so it is a small departure, but a famous and true one. The vision README offered Tallis's Canon or Old Hundredth as alternatives; my ranking is in Part 7.

### 1.4 Playford's *English Dancing Master* (1651): the tunes of the fair

John Playford's *The English Dancing Master* (1651) and its many later editions printed over a hundred country-dance tunes. These were the tunes of the tavern, the green and the fair: exactly the world Bunyan preached against, and exactly the sound of **Vanity Fair**, which Bunyan modelled on Stourbridge Fair near Cambridge. The full text and tunes are on IMSLP and many folk sites. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_English_Dancing_Master ; https://imslp.org/wiki/The_English_Dancing_Master_(Playford,_John)
- Tunes to know: *Parson's Farewell*, *Hearts Ease*, *Sellenger's Round*, *Gathering Peascods*, *Nonesuch*, *Jenny Pluck Pears*, *Rufty Tufty*, *Dargason*, *Greensleeves* (older, a ballad tune), *The Merry Milkmaids*, *Mage on a Cree*.
- **Broadside ballads** were sung to a handful of stock tunes: *Fortune my Foe* (the tune for murders and hangings), *Packington's Pound*, *Greensleeves*. The English Broadside Ballad Archive at UC Santa Barbara has recordings of many tunes. https://ebba.english.ucsb.edu/
- *Use:* in Vanity Fair, several Playford tunes played at once from different booths, a little too fast and a little out of tune with each other. It is busy and bright, the world's own music, and it stands in contrast to the hymn's lights. When Faithful is taken, the fair's music stops all at once, and that is the event. These are period tunes, free, and can be rendered in code (fiddle, pipe and tabor) or taken from public-domain recordings.

### 1.5 Viol consorts and Purcell

- **The consort of viols** was the chamber music of the godly gentry. Even Cromwell's household had music, and the Commonwealth did not ban it outside church. Its sound is soft, grave and reedy, bowed without vibrato.
- **John Jenkins** (1592 to 1678, who died the year *The Pilgrim's Progress* appeared) wrote fantasias and a pavan called **"The Bells"**, in which the viols imitate a peal. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Jenkins_(composer)
- **William Lawes** (killed 1645) wrote the stranger, darker consort setts. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lawes
- **Henry Purcell** (1659 to 1695) was writing while Bunyan wrote. His *Fantazias* for viols (1680) are the last great English consort music. His *Music for the Funeral of Queen Mary* (1695: a slow march for "flat trumpets" and drums, and "Thou knowest, Lord") is the most grave English music of the age. https://imslp.org/wiki/Category:Purcell,_Henry
- Scores are free on IMSLP. Performances are mostly copyrighted, but **Musopen** and **Wikimedia Commons** have some public-domain or CC0 recordings. Search https://musopen.org/music/?q=purcell and https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Henry_Purcell (audio).
- *Use:*
  - **The Interpreter's House and Palace Beautiful:** a viol-like texture (three or four soft bowed lines, no vibrato) is the sound of a godly house. It belongs to its time, and it is not a modern film score.
  - **Faithful's death and his chariot:** the Funeral March's idea of muffled trumpets and slow drums, written fresh, never quoted, is the right gravity for the martyr. Keep it very short and far off ("with sound of trumpet", `{242}`).

## Part 2. Vaughan Williams, Holst, Parry: the English pilgrim sound

**Ralph Vaughan Williams** lived with Bunyan for fifty years:
- incidental music for an amateur staging (Reigate, 1906, **unverified date**);
- the one-act *The Shepherds of the Delectable Mountains* (1922);
- a BBC radio score (1943);
- the **Fifth Symphony** (1943), built from material for the opera and dedicated to Sibelius. Its slow movement, *Romanza*, carries the music for Christian at the Cross: "He hath given me rest by his sorrow". Its last movement ends in a long, quiet D major light that many hear as the Celestial City;
- the "morality" ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** (Covent Garden, 1951, Festival of Britain). In it the pilgrim is renamed Pilgrim, and the psalm tune **York** opens and closes the work **(I believe York is the tune; unverified, check a score)**.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pilgrim%27s_Progress_(opera) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._5_(Vaughan_Williams)

*What it teaches.* The English pastoral way of scoring holiness:
- modal harmony (Dorian, Mixolydian, Aeolian);
- open fifths and parallel triads sliding past each other;
- a solo instrument rising over held strings: cor anglais or viola in the *Romanza*, solo violin in *The Lark Ascending*;
- a folk or psalm tune treated as sacred;
- a long, soft, unhurried close.
This is the "native language" the game's later levels should speak. Vaughan Williams' music is in copyright (he died in 1958): never use a recording or quote a phrase. Describe its *manner* to Lyria in plain words: "modal English pastoral strings, open fifths, a solo viola rising over a held chord".

- **Gustav Holst** (died 1934, so public domain in the UK since 2005): *The Hymn of Jesus* (1917), *Savitri*, and the tune **Cranham** ("In the bleak midwinter"). His scores are free; recordings are not, unless public domain.
- **Hubert Parry** (died 1918, public domain): **Repton** ("Dear Lord and Father of mankind", 1888) and "Jerusalem" (1916). Their tunes and harmonies are free, but they are Victorian and Edwardian church sound, not the 1600s. Repton's last line, rising to a hush, is a model for a gentle swell.
- https://hymnary.org/tune/repton_parry

## Part 3. Amazing Grace and the shape-note world

### 3.1 History

- **The words:** John Newton, former slave-ship captain, wrote them for New Year's Day 1773 at Olney, Buckinghamshire. They were printed in *Olney Hymns* (1779) as "Faith's Review and Expectation". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazing_Grace
- **The tune, New Britain:**
  - It is an anonymous American folk tune, pentatonic (five notes, no fourth or seventh). That is why a child can sing it after one hearing, and why the game's lights can sound it one note at a time with no wrong note possible.
  - It first appears as *Gallaher* and *St. Mary* in *Columbian Harmony* (1829) and *Virginia Harmony* (1831) **(unverified detail)**.
  - It was first joined to Newton's words by **William Walker** in ***The Southern Harmony*** (1835). Walker's book is shape-note, sold about 600,000 copies, and gave the tune to the South.
  - It entered B. F. White and E. J. King's ***The Sacred Harp*** (1844) as "New Britain".
  - Edwin Othello Excell's 1900 arrangement is the one most people now sing. https://hymnary.org/tune/new_britain
- **An honest note for the owner:** the hymn is a century after Bunyan and comes from another country. The game uses it as a found melody, not a period piece. That is a choice already made and a good one: no child knows Old Hundredth, and every child half-knows Amazing Grace. Everything round it can still come from Bunyan's own century.

### 3.2 Sacred Harp as a sound world

Shape-note singing is quite unlike polished choral singing:
- The four parts sit in a hollow square, facing in, and each takes a turn leading.
- The tune is in the tenor, sung by men and women together. The chords are open, built on fourths and fifths, often with no third.
- Parts cross. Everyone sings full voice, untrained, beating time with the hand.
- A song is first sung on its syllables (fa, sol, la, mi), then on the words.
- The "fuguing tunes" set voices chasing one another.
- Library of Congress overview: https://www.loc.gov/item/ihas.200185140/ (unverified URL; search "Sacred Harp" at loc.gov). Free scans: https://fasola.org . Listen: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=sacred+harp+new+britain and the Lomax field recordings at https://archive.org/search?query=sacred+harp (check each licence).
- Tunes worth knowing (all public domain): **Idumea** ("And am I born to die?"), **Wondrous Love** (*Southern Harmony*, 1835, minor, Dorian), **Kedron**, **Bourbon**, **The Promised Land** ("On Jordan's stormy banks I stand": the River!), **Northfield**, and **Wayfaring Stranger** (folk, 19th century, "I'm just a poor wayfaring stranger, travelling through this world of woe ... I'm only going over Jordan").

*What it gives the game.*
- **The River (Level 17).** *The Promised Land* and *Wayfaring Stranger* are literally songs of crossing Jordan into the City. Played as a single far voice, or as lights, they are the River's own songs. The hymn stays for the Gate.
- **Open fifths are the sound of "between".** A drone of a bare fifth, with no third, is neither glad nor sad. It is the right ground for the Valley of Humiliation and the Enchanted Ground. Adding the third at the right moment is a small, readable event: the sun coming up in harmony.
- **The singing school's "fa sol la" before the words** is the hymn-as-lights idea made literal: first the notes, then the meaning.

## Part 4. Games: how quiet, story-led games use music

| Game | Composer / sound | What it does that this game can use |
|---|---|---|
| **Journey** (2012) | Austin Wintory | A solo **cello is the player**. It enters quietly, grows through the game, and is joined by the whole orchestra only at the summit. The score is cut into stems that follow where you are. Your "chirp" (your one sound) is always tuned to the music's key, so whatever the player does sounds right. One theme returns in many forms, and the end credits song ("I Was Born for This") sets the theme to texts from many faiths. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journey_(2012_video_game) |
| **GRIS** (2018) | Berlinist | Colour and music come back together: each regained colour adds instruments to a piano-and-voice theme. Grief is a wordless solo voice. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gris_(video_game) |
| **Limbo / Inside** (2010, 2016) | Martin Stig Andersen | **Sound as music.** Hardly any tune at all: drones, the world's noises bent into tones. For *Inside*, Andersen played sounds through a human skull and re-recorded them, so the score has a muffled, bodily colour. The music's pulse is timed to the boy's breath. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inside_(video_game) |
| **Ori and the Blind Forest** (2015) | Gareth Coker | Leitmotifs for characters; one treble voice for the spirit tree. Big orchestral moments come only at a few story beats, with long ambient stretches between. |
| **Hollow Knight** (2017) | Christopher Larkin | Each region has its own theme. Layers of a piece are added as you go deeper (vertical layering). A viola or cello solo stands for the lonely knight. Silence in the hardest places. |
| **Spiritfarer** (2020) | Max LL | Warm, acoustic folk; music tied to the people you care for; one farewell theme per character. |
| **Alto's Adventure / Odyssey** (2015, 2018) | Torin Borrowdale | Ambient piano and pads, endless and calm. Weather and time of day change the sound. A "Zen mode" with no score. |
| **Sky: Children of the Light** (2019) | Vincent Diamante | Players play real instruments to each other. Candles of light are gathered. Music at gates and temples, wind elsewhere. |
| **Abzû** (2016) | Austin Wintory | Choir, harp and strings. The choir's texts come from old hymns and poems **(unverified which)**. The score swells when the player frees a part of the sea. |
| **Old Man's Journey** (2017) | Scntfc | Spare acoustic piano and strings; a life remembered as you walk. Sound gives the flashbacks. |
| **Outer Wilds** (2019) | Andrew Prahlow | **The closest thing to the hymn-as-lights.** Each of the scattered Travellers plays one instrument (banjo, harmonica, flute, drums, whistling, piano) at their own camp across the solar system. With a "signalscope" you can hear each one alone, far off, and find them by it. All of them play the same tune ("Travelers"). At the very end of the universe they gather and play it together, whole, for the first time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Wilds |

**Outer Wilds is the strongest case for the owner's plan.** A tune found in pieces in many places, and heard whole only at the end, is what makes its ending one of the most moving in games. That is Amazing Grace's path in this game: phrase by phrase in the lights, three remembered phrases, whole at the Cross and the Gate.

### Ways music can follow play, judged for a gentle walk

1. **Stingers on events** (a short phrase that plays once when something happens). Best fit; the game already does this (the beat at "the man began to run", the heartbeat). *Rule: every sting is pre-made with its fades inside it (`MUS_ONCE`), and never sounds under a voice.*
2. **Notes snapped to the key** (Journey's chirp). Every sound the child makes by touching should be in the key of the moment. The lights already do this: the hymn's notes, Level 2's minor wading notes, the leaps' G B D. **Extend it:** knocks, bird touches and glints in each level should take their pitch from the current tonal centre. The game becomes musical without music playing.
3. **Vertical layering** (the same piece with layers added or taken away). This fits a level's long build, such as the wading in Level 2. Keep to two or three layers at most.
4. **Re-ordering sections** (moving between parts of a piece). Not needed: the game is linear.
5. **Music heard from a place in the world** (Outer Wilds' Travellers, a radio). This is the most Bunyan-like method: the voice ahead in the Valley, the shepherds' pipe, the fair's booths, the City's bells. **Music that has a source in the world never breaks the rule "silence and the world's own sounds".**
6. **Ducking** (music sinking under a voice). Already built (`.42`, `.16` for the choir).
7. **Following the player's walking.** Avoid. The game's tempo is the walk, and music that speeds up with the child's finger turns it into a game. Journey ties its music to place, not speed.

### How children of 7 to 10 hear music

- Studies of children's emotional reading of music (Dalla Bella, Peretz and others, *Cognition*, 2001) found:
  - five-year-olds judge happy or sad by **tempo** only;
  - from about **six to eight**, children also use **mode** (major against minor) as adults do.
- The players are at the age where minor sounds sad and major sounds glad. Level 2's minor wading and the Cross's turn to major will be heard as meaning. Tempo matters even more: slow is grave, quick is glad. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0010-0277(00)00136-0
- Children react strongly to **sudden loud low sounds** (startle) and to **a single human voice**. Keep low drums soft and far (Apollyon, the Valley). Use the child treble voice as the most tender sound in the game.

## Part 5. Sacred minimalists, and chant from other traditions

### 5.1 Sacred minimalists

**Arvo Pärt (*tintinnabuli*).**
- The method: a melodic voice moves by step, while a second voice sounds only the notes of one triad, like a bell. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintinnabuli
- *Für Alina* (1976), *Spiegel im Spiegel* (1978) and *Fratres* (1977) are the pieces to know. The owner already chose "the manner of *Fratres*" for Level 1's run (build 527), so this language is already in the game.
- Tintinnabuli can be written in code: one stepwise line plus a triad line. It suits the far light: a high soft triad answering each step.
- Copyright: describe the method, never name it.

**Henryk Górecki.** Symphony No. 3, "Symphony of Sorrowful Songs" (1976): slow canons climbing out of the double basses, and a soprano. Grief that becomes peace. A model for the Valley of the Shadow and Faithful's death (far too large to use whole).

**John Tavener.** *The Lamb* (1982, Blake's words) for unaccompanied choir. A child-sized holy song: the melody, then its mirror image sung against it. A model for the Shining Ones' "ah".

**Morten Lauridsen** (*O Magnum Mysterium*, 1994) and **Eric Whitacre** (*Lux Aurumque*, 2000). Clustered chords of added notes, soft and glowing: the "light" sound of modern choirs. Lovely, but it is the sound of the 1990s and easily sentimental. Use it only as the Celestial City's furthest edge, if at all.

**What children make of it.** Slow, consonant, repetitive music with a single voice tends to calm children of this age. Long dissonant clusters can unsettle them. **(This is general music-education experience, not a study I can cite; unverified.)**

### 5.2 Chant and folk

**Gaelic psalm singing, Isle of Lewis.**
- A precentor gives out a line. The congregation answers, each singer adding their own turns, and the line swells into a slow, wavering, heterophonic sea. Common tunes are *Martyrdom*, *Coleshill*, *Stroudwater* and *Dundee*.
- It is lining out kept alive: the 1600s' own congregational sound, as close as we can get today.
- Recordings (Greentrax, *Scottish Tradition* series) are copyrighted. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_psalm_singing ; listen: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=gaelic+psalm+singing+lewis
- **Best fit:** House Beautiful's household at prayer, heard through a wall; or the hill Clear. A Lyria prompt can describe it.

**Russian Orthodox chant.**
- Znamenny chant: one line, deep basses, an octave drone.
- Alexander Kastalsky arranged it for choir (his *Requiem for Fallen Brothers*, 1917). Rachmaninov's *All-Night Vigil* (1915) is built on it.
- The **basso profundo** heard under a choir is the deepest sacred sound there is. It is far from Bunyan's Puritan world, so it is not a fit here except perhaps as a far edge of the Celestial City. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Znamenny_chant

**Georgian polyphony** (UNESCO Masterpiece, 2001). Three-voice songs with a drone and sharp, bright intervals. Wonderful, but exotic for this book. Leave it out. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_polyphony

**Japanese shakuhachi honkyoku.**
- The **komusō**, "monks of emptiness" of the Fuke sect, wandered Japan in basket hats that hid their faces, playing the bamboo flute as meditation ("blowing Zen"). Pieces include *Kyorei* ("empty bell"), *Tsuru no Sugomori* and *Shika no Tōne*. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honkyoku
- **Pilgrims who walked with their faces hidden, breathing one long tone at a time.** It is the closest musical picture of Christian there is: the hooded walker, his plain face.
- The sound is breath, a pitch bent by the lip, and silence between phrases. This is not about quoting Japan. The lesson is the *idea*: a single breathy wind tone with long silences can be "Christian walking". An English recorder or a low wooden flute played that way (no tune, long bent breaths) carries the same thing. Hasui and Yoshida (already named in the art direction) are its picture-world.

## Part 6. The sound and music script for all seventeen levels

### 6.1 The cast of sounds (one sound, one meaning, all game long)

| Who or what | Sound | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **The world** (the ground) | Rain, wind, birds, steps by surface, water, fire, bells far off | Always there; never covered by music for long |
| **The far light** | The **hymn's lights**: a soft bell-like sine with a long ring, one note each, in the hymn's key | Only on the lights. After the Cross, only three remembered phrases |
| **Christian's heart** | **One low bowed string** (cello or viol), alone, no vibrato; the run's grave strings (build 527) are its fullest form | Only at his inner turning points: the run from home, the Slough's fears, at the Cross, Doubting Castle's despair, the River |
| **The burden** | A **low drone with a faint creak** under his loneliest moments (Levels 1 to 5) | It stops with a cut, never a fade, when the burden falls, and never returns. The silence after it is the gift |
| **The helpers** (Evangelist, Help, Good-will, Interpreter, Watchful, Shepherds) | **One shared phrase**: the first line of **Old Hundredth**, or a short rising fourth then a falling step, on low soft strings or a hum, heard once as each comes | A child learns "this sound means someone kind is coming" (the vision's "1978 film's doubling") |
| **The tempters** (Worldly Wiseman, the Flatterer, By-ends, Vanity's merchants, the Enchanted Ground) | **Too-sweet music**: Level 3's tune; a pretty dance a little out of tune; a lullaby | Sweetness that sours or slows; never menace |
| **Danger** (Apollyon, Giant Despair, the Valley, the lions) | **Not music**: low drums felt more than heard (L1's heartbeat), the world's sounds stopping, a single far roar | The terror is the silence; then one deep sound |
| **The Shining Ones and the City** | **A wordless choir** led by **one boy treble** | Only three times: the Cross, a breath on the hill Clear, the Gate (with "Holy, holy, holy") |
| **Companions** (Pliable, Faithful, Hopeful) | **Two lines together**: Tallis's Canon as a round, a second voice entering a bar behind | Walking together sounds like two parts of one tune |
| **The second walking tune** (after the Cross) | **Monks Gate** (Bunyan's own "To be a pilgrim"), bare melody on a recorder or low wooden flute, then joined by a second voice once Faithful or Hopeful walks with him | Heard only at the start of a level's walk and once at its end, never under talk or lights |

### 6.2 Level by level

**1. City of Destruction** (as built). Rain, town and steps; the hymn's first phrases in lights; the run's grave strings over a faint heartbeat; the heartbeat as the neighbours come; the hymn whole at the gate (the first of three). *Add, free:*
- the burden's drone under the opening, faint;
- the helper's phrase once as Evangelist appears;
- the town's church bell far off once at the opening (Bunyan's Elstow bells), and never again until the City.

**2. Slough of Despond** (as built). Minor wading notes, drums and dark for the fears, Help's grip as a major chord. *Add:* the helper's phrase as Help steps out of the mist; at the steps, the lights' rising chord (already built). Nothing more: it is the best-scored level.

**3. Worldly Wiseman and the hill.** The too-sweet tune for Wiseman (built); the hymn bent flat off the way (built); under the hill, only rumble and fire, then the drone of a bare fifth. *Add:* the helper's phrase as Evangelist walks out of the haze; the low string (Christian's heart) on "Woe is me". At the gate: the lantern's hiss, the knocks, and silence before the door opens.

**4. The Wicket-Gate and the Interpreter's House.** Room-by-room beds (built, made in the browser). *Change:* make them a **viol-consort texture**, three soft bowed lines in an old mode, period-true for a godly house, and still very quiet. Room 5's welcome "Come in, come in" over a far choir? **No: keep the choir for the Cross.** A far fanfare of two natural trumpets instead. Rooms 6 and 7: only the world (a drip; thunder and the trumpet of the dream).

**5. The Cross** (as built, choir and kneel in). The burden's drone cut; silence; the hymn whole (the second of three); the treble and the choir with the Shining Ones; the leaps' G B D; "Blest cross!" in his own voice. *Add:* as he walks on, the **first notes of the second walking tune**, one phrase alone on the recorder: the new road's sound is born.

**6. Hill Difficulty, the lost roll, the lions.**
- **The climb:** steps on rock and his breath (the book: "from running he went to walking, and from walking to clambering"). No music.
- **The arbour:** birds, and a lullaby-ish drowsiness (the tempters' sweetness: he sleeps and loses the roll).
- **The search back:** silence and his steps.
- **The roll found** (`{107}`): the **first remembered hymn phrase**, one line, in the lights.
- **The lions:** two low growls, his heartbeat, the chains rattling when the porter calls. No score.

**7. Palace Beautiful.**
- **Evening:** the household's prayer heard through the wall in the Lewis manner (a precentor and a soft swelling answer, wordless or "ah"), or the viol consort.
- **Sunrise in the chamber Peace** (`{131}`): he sings. It stays his: spoken, then the second walking tune's first full statement, on the recorder alone, as he looks from the window toward the Delectable Mountains.

**8. Apollyon, in the Valley of Humiliation.**
- **The valley:** a bare-fifth drone and wind.
- **Apollyon:** the world's sounds stop; deep, slow, felt drums; his voice; the darts as hisses; **no melody at all**.
- **After:** the leaves of the tree of life, birds coming back one by one, then the helper's phrase in thanks. Silence is the score.

**9. Valley of the Shadow of Death.** Darkness, the pit's sounds, whispers (the book's fiends whispering blasphemies: keep them breath-like, no words a child can make out). Then, far ahead, **one voice lining out Psalm 23** ("Though I walk through the valley ..."). It is Faithful, though Christian does not yet know it. Use the 1650 Scottish Psalter words in Common Metre, to **Crimond** (as ruled; 1872, anachronistic) or to the period tune **French/Dundee** (1615) *(my preference: Bunyan's century)*. Day breaks: the **second remembered hymn phrase**, the sunrise of `{166}`.

**10. Faithful (and Talkative).** Two companions: the walking tune as **Tallis's Canon-style round**, two lines a bar apart, heard once as they fall into step. **Talkative's endless chatter** is a busy, empty tune, quick notes with no rest, like a jig that never cadences. It stops when he leaves, and the relief is the joke.

**11. Vanity Fair.**
- **The fair:** Playford tunes from every booth, overlapping, a little sour; bells of hawkers; crowd murmur.
- **The trial:** the fair falls silent, a single drum.
- **Faithful's death:** never shown. A far bell tolling once, then the chariot's **far trumpet**, two notes, rising out of hearing (`{242}`), and the treble's single held note, very far: the Shining Ones' sound, a breath of it, since a chariot from heaven came for him. *This is the only exception to the choir's three places; offer it to the owner, or leave the trumpet alone.*

**12. Hopeful, By-ends, the silver mine.** The walking tune, now **with Hopeful's second voice**. By-ends: a smug, rounded little tune (tempter family). **Demas at the silver mine:** a glittering, chiming lure (the too-sweet family, high and metallic). Lot's wife as a pillar: silence. The river of the water of life: gentle water and birds, no score.

**13. By-path Meadow and Doubting Castle.**
- **The meadow:** pleasant, then the storm and the flood, then the dark.
- **The dungeon:** no music for a long time, the Giant's cudgel thudding far off, water dripping. Christian's heart (the low string) once, in despair.
- **The key of Promise** (`{292}`, "almost break of day"): the **third remembered hymn phrase** as the key turns. The gates groan open on their own sounds, and they run.

**14. The Delectable Mountains.** The **shepherds' pipe**: a recorder or low flute playing slowly, with long breaths, in the honkyoku spirit. Sheep bells; wide wind. The hill Error and Mount Caution: silence. **The hill Clear:** through the glass, **ten seconds of the Cross's choir**, faint and far, lost when the hands shake. The interval card ("So I awoke ... And I slept, and dreamed again"): total silence.

**15. Ignorance and the Flatterer's net.** **The Flatterer:** a smooth, lovely tune that turns them gradually off the way (tempter family: pretty, then sour as the net falls). Then the Shining One with a whip of small cord. Ignorance: his own little cheerful whistle, unchanged all through, which stops for good at the end.

**16. The Enchanted Ground.** The **lullaby** of the air, sweet and slow, falling in pitch, the danger by sweetness. The two pilgrims keep awake by talking (the book), so their voices are the music. The walking tune, slowed and drowsy, picks up again as they come out.

**17. Beulah, the River and the Celestial City.**
- **Beulah:** birdsong in full, flowers, the City's **bells far off** (the first since Level 1's town), the Shining Ones walking (light, no choir yet).
- **The River:** water, Christian's heart (the low string) at its lowest. Hopeful's "I feel the bottom, and it is good" is the **last remembered hymn phrase**, under the water's roar. On the far bank, *The Promised Land* or *Wayfaring Stranger* sung by one far voice? *No: keep it to the hymn.*
- **The Gate:** the King's trumpets play **Amazing Grace whole** (the third time), "all the bells in the city rang again for joy", and the choir, now with words, "Holy, holy, holy". Then the gate shuts and there is silence. (Ignorance's end follows in silence: no music for the dark door.)

## Part 8. Ten sample Lyria prompts in plain words

No composer or piece is named. Each says how it should start and end, so it can be cut and laid in. Keep each under 30 s with `lyria-3-clip-preview` (about 4 cents) unless the piece needs its length. **Each costs money: say the cost and wait for the owner's yes.**

1. **The burden's drone (Levels 1 to 5).** "A single very low bowed string note held for thirty seconds, with a faint creak of rope and wood in it, as if something heavy is being carried. No melody, no drums, no other instruments. Soft, tired, steady. It begins and ends in silence."
2. **Christian's heart (a solo string).** "One cello alone, no vibrato, playing a slow, plain, stepwise melody in an old minor mode, like someone praying quietly to himself. Long notes and long silences between phrases. Very quiet, close and intimate, a small room. Twenty seconds, ending on a held low note that fades."
3. **The helper's call.** "A short, warm phrase of four slow notes played by low strings softly in unison, rising then settling, like a kind friend arriving. Simple, old-fashioned, hymn-like, no drums. Six seconds, with a soft fade in and out."
4. **The godly house (Level 4, Palace Beautiful).** "Three or four soft bowed instruments of an old consort, gentle and reedy, no vibrato, playing slow interweaving lines in an old English mode by candlelight. Grave and peaceful. Very quiet, a background in a quiet room. Forty-five seconds, looping smoothly."
5. **Lining out (Palace Beautiful through a wall).** "A single man's voice sings a slow line of a plain psalm tune on 'ah', then a small congregation of untrained voices answers, very slowly, each singer adding their own little turns, so the line swells and wavers like a sea. Heard as if through a stone wall from the next room. Unaccompanied. Forty seconds."
6. **The second walking tune (one statement).** "A wooden recorder plays a simple, bright, striding folk melody in three time, plain and brave like a traveller setting out at dawn, alone at first, then a second recorder joins a bar later in a round. Light, outdoor, birds far off. Twenty-five seconds, starting and ending cleanly."
7. **Vanity Fair.** "A crowded old market fair: several small groups of fiddles, pipes and drums each playing a different lively country dance at the same time from different stalls, slightly out of tune with each other, with crowd chatter and hawkers' bells. Bright, busy, a little too much. Forty seconds, looping."
8. **The shepherds' pipe (Delectable Mountains).** "A single low breathy wooden flute plays long slow tones with bent pitches and a lot of breath, with long silences between them, on a high open mountainside with wind and far sheep bells. Meditative, peaceful, no melody to speak of. Forty-five seconds."
9. **The Enchanted Ground's lullaby (danger by sweetness).** "A sweet, drowsy, slow lullaby played by soft harp and a high gentle music box, warm and pretty but slowly sinking lower and lower in pitch and slowing down, as if falling asleep. Thirty seconds, ending almost stopped."
10. **The Gate's choir with words (Level 17; the build 549 manner grown).** "A single boy treble sings a long, pure note, then a large unaccompanied choir of men, women and children blooms beneath it, singing slowly and with great joy the words 'Holy, holy, holy', in a wide warm major, with church bells pealing far off. One great swell, then it fades into quiet light. Seventy seconds."

## Part 9. Recommendations, ranked

1. **Adopt the cast of sounds (6.1) as a rule for every level.** One sound, one meaning: helpers, tempters, danger, the light, the Shining Ones, the burden, his heart. It costs nothing, and it lets a seven-year-old read the story by ear.
2. **Second walking tune: Monks Gate**, bare, on a recorder. It is Bunyan's own "To be a pilgrim", born as he walks from the Cross, and doubled into a round when a companion walks with him (Tallis's Canon's idea).
3. **The voice ahead in the Valley:** a lone voice lining out Psalm 23 in the dark, revealed later as Faithful's. Free if rendered in code; a hum-and-voice Lyria clip if not. The single most "Bunyan's world" musical moment available.
4. **Pitch every touch to the moment's key** (Journey's chirp). Free, and it makes the whole game musical without adding music.
5. **Vanity Fair from Playford.** Period-true, free tunes, the world's own music, stopping dead at Faithful's arrest.
6. **The burden's drone cut at the Cross** (Levels 1 to 5, free in code). The child will feel the loss of a sound they never noticed was there.
7. **Bells only twice:** a far church bell at Level 1's opening (Bunyan's Elstow bells) and the City's peal at the end.

**Not recommended:** any recording or quotation of Vaughan Williams, Pärt, Tavener or modern choirs (all in copyright); Georgian or Orthodox chant (beautiful, but foreign to the book's world); music that follows the child's walking speed; any choir outside the three places (Faithful's chariot is offered only as a breath, for the owner to decide).
