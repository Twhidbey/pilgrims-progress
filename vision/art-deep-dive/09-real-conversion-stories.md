# 9. The road in real lives: how *The Pilgrim's Progress* maps onto true stories of being saved

Research report for the owner (art deep dive, researcher 9 of 12). Nothing in the game was changed and nothing was spent.

**How far to trust it.** Most primary texts could not be opened from this session (Project Gutenberg, CCEL and archive.org are outside the allowed network), so Bunyan's *Grace Abounding*, James's *Varieties* and Fussell were checked only through search summaries. Each item is marked:

- **[V]**: confirmed in this session by a search result from a solid source (a publisher, a scholarly review, a reputable history magazine).
- **[M]**: from memory of standard biographies. Widely told and believed accurate, but **check against the primary text before quoting it in the game**.
- **[?]**: uncertain or disputed. Do not use without checking.

Section numbers like `{93}` are Bunyan's, from `book/pilgrims-progress-part-one.txt`, checked there.

## 1. Why this matters for the game

The owner wants the story to connect to how real people have been saved. That fits Bunyan well, because the allegory came out of a real life. *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners* (1666) is Bunyan's own account of his conversion. *The Pilgrim's Progress* (1678) turns that same experience into a dream of a road. For 350 years readers have recognised their own lives in it, which is why it was translated into about 200 languages [V, Hofmeyr]. Missions counted it second only to the Bible in number of translations [V, review of Hofmeyr].

So when the game shows the Slough, the burden or the Cross, it is not showing only a fairy-tale landscape. It shows things that real people (a tinker in Bedford, a slave-ship captain, a lawyer in Germany, a boy in a snowstorm) said happened to them. The task is to let a child of 7 to 10 feel that, gently, without preaching and without frightening.

## 2. Bunyan himself: the allegory read against *Grace Abounding*

Bunyan was a brazier (a tinker) from Elstow near Bedford, a soldier in the Parliamentary army as a teenager, married young to a poor woman whose dowry was two devotional books. He went through about five years of extreme spiritual anguish (roughly 1650 to 1655), joined John Gifford's independent congregation in Bedford, became a preacher, and was jailed for about twelve years (1660 to 1672) for preaching without a licence. In jail he began the book [M; the dates are standard].

| Book episode | Bunyan's own life in *Grace Abounding* | Mark |
|---|---|---|
| The man in rags with a book, crying "What shall I do?" `{10}` | His wife's two books (*The Plain Man's Pathway to Heaven*, *The Practice of Piety*) first stirred him. He became earnest and outwardly religious, then found that was not enough. | [M] |
| The voice that turns him | On a Sunday, in the middle of a game of "cat" (tipcat) on the village green, a voice seemed to dart into his soul: "Wilt thou leave thy sins and go to heaven, or have thy sins and go to hell?" | [M]; the search confirmed the passage is in the book but not its wording |
| Evangelist | John Gifford, a former Royalist officer turned pastor of the Bedford congregation. Bunyan sat under his preaching, and Gifford is widely held to be the model for Evangelist. | [V, Wicket Gate magazine; Wikipedia's Gifford article] |
| The wicket-gate, seen far off | Bunyan saw in a waking vision the poor people of Bedford on the sunny side of a high mountain, himself shut out in the cold by a wall, and at last found a narrow gap he squeezed through. This is very nearly the wicket-gate and the wall. | [M]; a famous passage, check before quoting |
| The people who already walk the way | He came upon three or four poor women of Bedford sitting at a door in the sun, talking of the new birth "as if joy did make them speak". They were members of Gifford's congregation. | [V] that the women were Gifford's people; wording [M] |
| The Slough of Despond, Doubting Castle, the Valley of the Shadow `{29}`, `{155}`, `{283}` | Years of terror: that he had committed the unpardonable sin; that he had "sold Christ" like Esau selling his birthright; that the steeple or the bell he loved to ring would fall on him for his sin (he gave up bell-ringing for fear). Every terror of the Valley ("voices", "whisperings" he thought were his own) is his own experience. | [M] |
| Help, and the steps through the Slough | He found Luther's commentary on Galatians "most fit for a wounded conscience", as if written out of his own heart. A book was the steps under the mire. | [M] |
| The burden falls at the Cross `{93}`, `{94}` | Walking in a field, the sentence came: "Thy righteousness is in heaven." He saw that his righteousness was Christ himself, not his good or bad days, "and now did my chains fall off my legs indeed". | [M]; a famous line, check before quoting |
| Faithful, Hopeful, the companions | His congregation, and the women at the door. | [M] |
| Vanity Fair, the trial, Doubting Castle | His own arrest, trial and long imprisonment. Leaving his family, above all his blind daughter Mary, was, he wrote, like pulling the flesh from his bones. | [M] |
| The key called Promise `{292}` | Over and over in *Grace Abounding* a single Bible sentence is what opens his despair. Christian's key is a verse. | [M] |

**For the game:** Bunyan's own life is the first and best "true story". Every trial the child walks through was first a real fear in a real man, and the book was written in a prison cell. A child can hold that.

## 3. Other lives that match the road

Below, each life is summarised briefly with the part of the book it best fits. The aim is a quiet echo, not a claim that their experience was identical.

### John Newton (1725 to 1807), the game's own hymn
A sailor and later a slave-trader, known for blasphemy. In March 1748 (10 March Old Style, 21 March New Style) a violent storm in the North Atlantic nearly sank his ship, the *Greyhound*. He worked the pumps, then took the helm for hours, and cried out for mercy [V, Christian History Institute, Ligonier]. He had been reading *The Christian's Pattern* (an English version of Thomas à Kempis's *Imitation of Christ*), which he said sowed the seed of his conversion [V, Museum of the Bible, Wikipedia]. He did not count himself a settled believer until later [V], and he went on in the slave trade for several years more. Later, as a minister in Olney, he wrote "Amazing Grace" (published 1779). Decades afterwards he publicly repented of the trade and supported Wilberforce in ending it [M; *Thoughts upon the African Slave Trade*, 1788].
- **Fits:** the Slough (sinking, a hand from outside); the slow road after the gate (he was not finished at the storm; nor is Christian at the Cross); "I once was lost, but now am found" is the Cross.
- **Gentle telling:** a sailor who did wrong things, nearly lost in a storm, cried to God, and became a man who wrote the song you hear on the road. The slave trade must be named truthfully but briefly, with his later repentance; for 7 to 10 year olds "he carried people as prisoners on his ship, which was very wrong, and later he worked to stop it" is honest and bearable.

### Augustine of Hippo (354 to 430)
A brilliant teacher who could not break with his old life. In a garden in Milan in 386, weeping, he heard a child's voice from a nearby house singing "take up and read" (*tolle lege*). He opened Paul's letters at Romans 13:13-14 and "all the darkness of doubt vanished away" [M; *Confessions* Book VIII, well established].
- **Fits:** the burden and the weeping of `{10}`; the book in Christian's hand; the voice that turns him. A child's voice that changed a great man's life is a gift to a children's game.

### Martin Luther (1483 to 1546)
In a thunderstorm in 1505 a lightning strike threw him down and in terror he vowed to become a monk [M]. In the monastery he confessed for hours and found no peace; he feared God as a judge. His "tower experience" (the date is debated, about 1515 to 1519) was understanding Romans 1:17, "the just shall live by faith": the righteousness of God is given, not earned, and he felt "born again and entered paradise through open gates" [M].
- **Fits:** the burning hill of Level 3 exactly. Mr. Legality and the law that only makes the burden heavier; the fire and thunder; Evangelist's "The just shall live by faith" (in the book at `{56}`). Luther's commentary was then the book that helped Bunyan.

### John Wesley (1703 to 1791)
A strict, hard-working clergyman who went to Georgia as a missionary and came back feeling he had failed. On a sea voyage a storm terrified him, and he was struck by the calm of Moravian families singing through it [M]. On 24 May 1738, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, while someone read Luther's preface to Romans, "I felt my heart strangely warmed" [M; his Journal, well known].
- **Fits:** the Interpreter's House (being taught, the fire that grace keeps burning `{80}` to `{82}`); and the Cross as a warming, not a lightning bolt. Note the chain: Paul to Augustine, Luther to Bunyan and Wesley. People were helped across by others' words, as Help draws Christian out.

### William Cowper (1731 to 1800)
Newton's friend and fellow hymn writer at Olney. He suffered severe depression, attempted suicide in 1763, was cared for in an asylum at St Albans, and there found faith reading Romans 3:25. Despair returned for much of the rest of his life; he came to believe he was shut out from God, yet wrote "God moves in a mysterious way" and "There is a fountain filled with blood" [M]. He also praised Bunyan in his poem *Tirocinium* without naming him, "lest so despised a name should move a sneer" [M].
- **Fits:** Doubting Castle and Giant Despair `{283}`, honestly: not every real pilgrim got out quickly. **Handle with great care:** suicide must not be mentioned to the game's age group. If used at all, "a gentle poet who often felt very sad and afraid, and whose friend Newton never stopped visiting him" is enough. Probably better kept for an adult "notes" page.

### Charles Spurgeon (1834 to 1892)
At 15, troubled for years, he was turned by a snowstorm on Sunday 6 January 1850 into a small Primitive Methodist chapel in Colchester. The preacher did not come; a thin man, perhaps a shoemaker or tailor, preached on Isaiah 45:22, "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth", and called to the boy in the corner: "Young man, you look very miserable... Look! Look! Look!" [M; Spurgeon's *Autobiography*, widely told]. Spurgeon later said he had read *The Pilgrim's Progress* about a hundred times [M].
- **Fits:** the Cross exactly: Christian "looked, and looked again" `{94}`. A child in a snowstorm who looked and was lightened is one of the best stories for 7 to 10 year olds.

### Hudson Taylor (1832 to 1905)
At 17, alone at home, he idly picked up a gospel tract in his father's library, meaning to read only the story; the phrase "the finished work of Christ" struck him and he knelt. His mother, about 80 miles away, had at that same hour shut herself in to pray for him until she was sure [M; *Hudson Taylor's Spiritual Secret* and his own retelling]. He went on to found the China Inland Mission.
- **Fits:** the Cross ("It is finished"); and the unseen helpers: someone praying far off, as the Shining Ones come unseen.

### Isaac Watts (1674 to 1748) and Amy Carmichael (1867 to 1951): children
Watts came to faith at about 15 [M] and later wrote hymns for children (*Divine Songs*, 1715). Carmichael came to faith as a girl at a children's mission at Harrogate (about 15 [M]). As a girl in Belfast she helped a ragged old woman with her bundle through the street while respectable people stared, and the words of 1 Corinthians 3:12-14 seemed to flash to her [M]. She later spent 55 years in India rescuing children.
- **Fits:** being laughed at (the neighbours who mock him in Level 1, the scorners of Vanity Fair). Carmichael's story is a child's story: doing the right thing while others stare.

### Sadhu Sundar Singh (1889 to about 1929)
A Sikh boy from the Punjab whose beloved mother died when he was 14. In grief and anger he burned a Bible in public. Days later, in December 1904, he resolved to throw himself in front of the dawn train unless God showed himself; before dawn he saw a light and Jesus in it, and he became a wandering Christian holy man (*sadhu*) [M]. His family cast him out and, by his account, tried to poison him [M].
- **Fits:** the light in Level 1 (the far light he follows); leaving home and family who will not come; Faithful-like faithfulness under persecution. **Care:** the train must not be told to children; "he was so sad he did not want to live, and he prayed through the night" is the most a child version should say, and better left out.

### Pandita Ramabai (1858 to 1922)
An Indian scholar of Sanskrit, given the title Pandita, widowed young with a baby daughter. In England she was baptised (1883) and later wrote that she had found a religion but not yet Christ; a later reading (she named a book of a Christian's experience) brought her personal faith years after [M]. She founded the Mukti Mission for widows and orphaned girls and translated the Bible into Marathi [M].
- **Fits:** the long road after the gate: the Interpreter's House, teaching that comes slowly. Also Mercy and Christiana of Part Two (not in this game).

### Corrie ten Boom (1892 to 1983)
A Dutch watchmaker who with her family hid Jewish people from the Nazis. Arrested in 1944, she and her sister Betsie were sent to Ravensbrück, where Betsie died after telling her "there is no pit so deep that He is not deeper still" [M; *The Hiding Place*]. After the war, in a church in Munich, she met one of the camp guards, who asked her forgiveness; she could not feel it until she prayed, then shook his hand [M; she told it many times].
- **Fits:** Vanity Fair (faithfulness under unjust judges), Doubting Castle (a prison, and the key called Promise `{292}`), and Faithful's death softened. The forgiveness story is suitable for children if told gently.

### C. S. Lewis (1898 to 1963)
An atheist Oxford don who admitted God in Trinity Term 1929, "perhaps the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England" [M; *Surprised by Joy*]. In 1931, after a long night talk with Tolkien and Hugo Dyson, he came to believe in Christ on a ride to Whipsnade Zoo in his brother's motorcycle sidecar: "When we set out I did not believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God, and when we reached the zoo I did." His first Christian book, *The Pilgrim's Regress* (1933), deliberately imitates Bunyan [M].
- **Fits:** Pliable and Obstinate in reverse (a man argued with until he could not refuse); the way taken without knowing quite when the gate was passed. For children Lewis is the Narnia man, which is a doorway.

### Malcolm Muggeridge, Chuck Colson, Joni Eareckson Tada, Rosaria Butterfield, Nabeel Qureshi (modern)
- **Muggeridge** (1903 to 1990), a sharp-tongued journalist and doubter, moved toward faith partly through meeting Mother Teresa (his BBC film *Something Beautiful for God*, 1969), and was received into the Catholic Church in 1982 [M]. Fits Vanity Fair (a man of the fair who walked out of it).
- **Colson** (1931 to 2012), Nixon's "hatchet man", was given C. S. Lewis's *Mere Christianity* by a businessman friend, Tom Phillips, in 1973, wept in his car, went to prison over Watergate, and founded Prison Fellowship [M]. Fits the Interpreter's House (a friend reading aloud) and the prison of Bunyan himself.
- **Joni Eareckson Tada** (born 1949) broke her neck in a diving accident in 1967 at 17, wanted to die, raged and despaired for about two years, and came through to a faith that now serves disabled people worldwide (Joni and Friends) [M]. Fits the Valley of the Shadow and the Hill Difficulty. Children can hear "she was hurt and could not walk, and was very angry and sad, and God met her there."
- **Rosaria Butterfield**, a professor hostile to Christianity, was befriended over two years of dinners by a pastor, Ken Smith, and his wife (*The Secret Thoughts of an Unlikely Convert*, 2012) [M]. Fits Help and the Interpreter: patient hospitality, not argument.
- **Nabeel Qureshi** (1983 to 2017), a devout American Muslim, came to Christian faith after years of debate with his friend David Wood and, he wrote, a series of dreams (*Seeking Allah, Finding Jesus*, 2014) [M]. His story involves great grief to his parents and must be told with deep respect for his family and their faith; probably not for the children's game.

## 4. How the book itself worked in real lives

- **Missions and Africa.** Isabel Hofmeyr's *The Portable Bunyan: A Transnational History of The Pilgrim's Progress* (Princeton, 2004) follows the book through about 200 languages, 80 of them African; translators and readers reworked it to fit their own landscapes, and the Xhosa minister Tiyo Soga translated it into Xhosa (1868) [V, Princeton UP, reviews]. Readers in Africa often treated it not as a foreign book but as their own story [M, Hofmeyr's argument]. **Takeaway for the game:** the road belongs to every people; the game need not make it English only.
- **Soldiers in the First World War.** Paul Fussell's *The Great War and Modern Memory* (1975) argues that *The Pilgrim's Progress* was one of the few books that nearly every British soldier knew, and that they described the front in its words: the Slough of Despond for the mud of Flanders, the Valley of the Shadow of Death, Apollyon [M; the search confirmed Fussell uses Bunyan in reading soldiers' writing, e.g. a batman thinking of Christian's "certificate", but did not confirm the chapter's wording]. **Takeaway:** the Slough is the image real people reached for in the worst mud in history. The game's Slough is in good company.
- **Little Women** (Louisa May Alcott, 1868). The March girls play pilgrims from the first chapter ("Playing Pilgrims", each with a burden in a bundle), and chapters are named for the book: "Beth Finds the Palace Beautiful", "Amy's Valley of Humiliation", "Jo Meets Apollyon", "Meg Goes to Vanity Fair" [M, but confident]. Each girl's trial is a child's trial: shyness, vanity, a temper, wanting nice things. **This is the best model for the game:** Alcott showed 150 years ago that the trials can be ordinary childhood things.
- **Slaves, abolitionists, America.** The book was widely read in the American colonies and among enslaved and free Black Christians; the image of a pilgrim fleeing to a promised country fit spirituals and the Underground Railroad [?: plausible and often claimed; get a source, e.g. Hofmeyr's chapters on the Black Atlantic, before quoting]. Abraham Lincoln is often said to have read it as a boy among his few books [? : commonly repeated, check before use].
- **Prisoners.** Written in Bedford jail; Colson's Prison Fellowship and many prison ministries use it [M]. Bunyan's prison is the book's birthplace.

## 5. The psychology: why the road fits so many lives

**William James**, *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), Lectures VI and VII "The Sick Soul" and VIII "The Divided Self", uses Bunyan (beside Tolstoy) as a classic case [V, table of contents]. James contrasts the "healthy-minded", who need to be born only once, with "sick souls", who must be "twice-born": they feel the world's evil and their own sin deeply, are divided against themselves, and find peace through a crisis of surrender [V]. Christian is the twice-born pilgrim; Pliable, who turns back at the first mud, is a picture of easy enthusiasm without depth.

**Lewis Rambo**, *Understanding Religious Conversion* (Yale, 1993), describes conversion as seven overlapping stages: **context, crisis, quest, encounter, interaction, commitment, consequences** [M; standard summary]. Laid over the book:

| Rambo stage | In the book | In the game |
|---|---|---|
| Context | the City of Destruction | Level 1's rainy town |
| Crisis | the book, the burden, weeping `{10}` | the opening picture |
| Quest | fleeing, the Slough | Levels 1 to 2 |
| Encounter | Evangelist, Help, Good-will | the hands that are offered |
| Interaction | the Interpreter's House | Level 4 |
| Commitment | the Cross `{93}` | Level 5 |
| Consequences | the rest of the road: hills, lions, battles | Levels 6 onward |

The lesson for the game: real stories rarely end at one moment. Newton kept sailing; Ramabai waited years; Cowper never fully left his castle; Bunyan's agonies went on after his first relief. The book is honest about this: the Cross is at `{93}`, but Doubting Castle is at `{283}` and the River nearly drowns Christian at `{391}`. The game can be honest too, gently.

## 7. Recommendations for the game (ages 7 to 10)

Each is an idea; anything not in Bunyan is a departure and needs the owner's yes. None needs paid work except optional voices (about a cent a line).

**1. Keep the story itself unchanged.** The real stories should sit beside the road, never interrupt it. The owner's rules hold: the storyteller only tells the story; nothing pops up mid-level; the world stays quiet.

**2. Optional "True stories" in the book (recommended first).** The title is a book. Add a quiet back page, "Others who walked the road", reached from the contents (like the darker rooms switch) and from the pause menu after a level is finished. One short card per level, unlocked when the level is done, never forced:
- Level 1: **Bunyan** himself: "The man who wrote this story was a tinker. One day, playing a game on the village green, he felt God speak to his heart." (Plus: he wrote it in prison.)
- Level 2: **Newton and the storm**, linked to the hymn the child has been hearing: "A sailor who had done many wrong things was caught in a great storm. He cried to God for mercy. Years later he wrote the song you hear along the road."
- Level 3: **Luther** in the thunderstorm and the "just shall live by faith" line that Evangelist speaks.
- Level 4: **Augustine** and the child's voice ("Take up and read"), since the Interpreter's House is about reading pictures and words; or **Wesley's warmed heart** for the fire room.
- Level 5: **Spurgeon in the snow** ("Look!"), pairing exactly with Christian who "looked, and looked again".
Later levels: Corrie ten Boom (Vanity Fair, Doubting Castle), Amy Carmichael (being laughed at), Joni (Hill Difficulty), Hudson Taylor's praying mother (the Palace Beautiful).

**Form:** a single painted page in the game's layered look, one plain picture drawn in code (a snowy chapel door with one lit window; a ship on a dark sea; a tinker's green), three to five short sentences in Bunyan-plain English, optionally read aloud by the storyteller. No photographs of real people (avoids likeness issues and keeps the look). Dates kept simple ("about 270 years ago").

**3. A one-line frame at the very start or end (gentle, optional).** Bunyan's own "I dreamed a dream" already frames the game. At the last level's end, or on the book's last page: "Many people have walked this road. Perhaps you will too." Quiet, inviting, not a demand. Owner to decide.

**4. Make each trial something a child knows** (the Alcott method). Without changing a word of Bunyan's, the art and the child's acts can carry ordinary feelings:
- *Fear:* the Slough's darkness and drums already do this. A true-story card can say: "Have you ever felt stuck and scared? Even grown-ups do."
- *Being laughed at:* the mockers in Level 1, Vanity Fair. Carmichael's story fits.
- *Giving up:* Pliable. The choice "Go back, as Pliable did" already lets a child feel it; the game's kindness (nothing is lost) is right.
- *Feeling alone:* "So Christian was left alone." A hand reaches (Help, Evangelist, Good-will). The game already makes the child take each hand; that IS the true-story pattern: almost every real conversion above came through someone reaching out.
- *Saying sorry and being forgiven:* the Cross.

**5. Honour the hymn's author.** The game uses "Amazing Grace" as its discovered tune and saves it whole for the Cross. Newton's story is the natural true-story card for Level 5 or for the end card after the Cross ("The song you heard was written by John Newton..."), and it is the most direct bridge between the game and a real life.

**6. Accuracy and respect rules for any card.**
- Quote only from primary texts checked word for word (as the Bible text already is). Items marked [M] above need checking first; Project Gutenberg (`gutenberg.org`) would need to be allowed in the environment to check *Grace Abounding*, *Confessions*, Wesley's *Journal* and James.
- Never imply that every real conversion is sudden or the same. Some cards should show slow roads (Ramabai, Lewis, Newton after the storm).
- Leave out suicide, abuse and graphic suffering (Cowper's attempts, Sundar Singh's train, the camps' details). Name the slave trade truthfully but briefly, with Newton's later repentance and work against it.
- Treat other faiths with respect: Sundar Singh's and Qureshi's families, Ramabai's Hindu learning. Tell what the person found, never mock what they left.
- Keep living people out (Joni, Rosaria Butterfield) unless the owner is comfortable; the dead are simpler.
- Avoid stories with disputed details (Lincoln's reading, specific slave-reading claims) until sourced.

**7. For parents and older readers.** A separate plain page (or a note in `vision/`) could hold the fuller and harder stories (Cowper, Colson, Qureshi, Fussell's soldiers, Hofmeyr's Africa) for adults who want them, kept out of the child's path.
