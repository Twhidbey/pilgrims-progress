## 1. Why animation background painting is the right school for this game

A background painter's problem is the same as this game's. A painted world has to:
1. hold still while a small figure crosses it,
2. never compete with that figure,
3. tell the audience where to look in the half-second a shot lasts, and
4. stay one coherent world across 80 minutes and hundreds of separate paintings.

Easel painters solve problem 3 in one picture. Background departments solve all four over a whole film. The game has 17 levels, each a long horizontal pan with a figure walking across it. That is exactly the form of a feature film's background "pan" painting. Eyvind Earle's *Sleeping Beauty* pans ran up to about 15 feet long [V, [Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2024/01/the-art-of-sleeping-beauty-65-years-later/)].

The game's look is already close to the tradition: layered silhouettes, one tone per layer, pale far and dark near, haze between, and one far light. What it lacks is the *system* a studio puts around a look so it survives many hands and many months: a colour script, value keys, edge rules and a shape vocabulary. That system is what this report is about.

## 2. The masters, and what each teaches this game

### 2.1 Michaël Dudok de Wit: *Father and Daughter* (2000) and *The Red Turtle* (2016)

**The single closest model for this game.**

*Father and Daughter* runs about 8.5 minutes and has no words. A girl watches her father row away across a polder. All her life she cycles back to the same dyke to look for him. Old at last, she walks out onto the dried seabed and finds his boat.

What the film is made of:
- **Silhouettes and no faces.** It is drawn entirely in silhouette, with no facial detail at all [V, [Short of the Week](https://www.shortoftheweek.com/2008/02/10/father-and-daughter); [Wikipedia](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_and_Daughter_(film))]. It won the Oscar for Best Animated Short in 2001 [V].
- **Charcoal and pencil in chiaroscuro.** The landscape is an imagined polder that changes as she ages. One essay calls the landscape the film's main symbol [V, [mdwiki](https://www.mdwiki.org/wiki/Michael_Dudok_de_Wit); [UAL essay](https://bzhu.myblog.arts.ac.uk/files/2026/01/ZHU-BInbin-1.2-fiinal-essay.pdf)]. The figures are digitally coloured over drawn tones, in sepia-to-grey washes with long cast shadows [M].
- **Four years of part-time work** [V, Wikipedia].

Its structure is the important part, and it is easy to see by watching it [M/J]:

1. **One place, revisited.** The dyke, the one tree and the poplars along the road come back again and again. The film never needs a new location to move the story on. Story is told by **what changes in a fixed frame**: season, weather, time of day, the length of shadows, the figure's age, her bicycle, then her children's bicycles.
2. **One horizon, very low**, often about a fifth of the way up. The sky is the stage. Figures are small and dark against it.
3. **Cycling as rhythm.** The repeated walk and bicycle cycles across the frame are the film's pulse. The *Danube Waves* waltz is matched to them, and the motion is calm, regular and hypnotic. The game's hold-to-walk is the same thing.
4. **Light as weather and as emotion.** Low sun throws shadows many times the figures' length. Rain, snow and fog are each one wash over the whole frame. The last sequence is golden and hazy as she finds the boat.
5. **The cast shadow as a second drawing.** Long thin shadows across a flat ground describe the ground's plane and the sun's direction with no rendering at all.
6. **Extreme economy.** Often only three things are in frame: sky, ground strip and figure. When a tree appears, it means something.

*The Red Turtle*, his feature with Studio Ghibli, keeps the method at length. The palette is muted and grisaille-like, inspired by Japanese ink and watercolour prints [V, [CS Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/The-Culture/Movies/2017/0120/The-Red-Turtle-has-a-quietly-breathtaking-delicacy)]. Nights are monochrome, and one scarlet turtle against dark blue sea lands with force [V, [BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/reviews/film-week-red-turtle-dream-desert-island-movie)]. The colour board moves from the island's muted colours toward richer blues, reds and greens as the hero's arc grows [V, [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/features/the-story-behind-silent-oscar-animation-hopeful-the-red-turtle-/5111635.article)]. That is the colour script principle: **colour is rationed and spent where the story turns.** Julien De Man is credited with backgrounds and the colour script [V, IMDb via search].

**For this game:** Bunyan's road is a journey, not a return. But the game already has two fixed points that work like the dyke: **the far light**, present on every screen, and **Christian's silhouette and burden**. Every level can be read as "the same figure, the same light, the world around them changed." That is Father and Daughter's grammar.

### 2.2 Lotte Reiniger: *The Adventures of Prince Achmed* (1926)

Reiniger animated cut-out figures of card and thin lead under a camera, frame by frame, as backlit silhouettes [V, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotte_Reiniger); [BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/features/scissors-make-films-lotte-reiniger-creating-her-magical-animations)]. Walter Ruttmann and Walter Türck made the dreamlike backgrounds, with effects by Berthold Bartosch and Carl Koch [V]. Some sources credit her team with an early multiplane set-up [V, one source; treat as contested].

Lessons [M/J]:
- **The silhouette must read in profile.** Reiniger's figures are designed so that hands, noses, hems and props stand clear of the body outline: a hand held away from the robe, a staff not crossing the body. A silhouette lives or dies on its **negative spaces**. This is the game's FLAT FEET rule, extended to the whole figure.
- **Lace against mass.** Her cut-outs pair big solid blacks with fine filigree (branches, lattices, hair). The contrast between filled mass and open fretwork *is* the style.
- **Backgrounds in graded grey and tinted light, never black.** The figures are the only true black. Behind them are layers of graded translucency, and each act is tinted by toning the film stock (amber, blue, green). Tinting by act is a colour script before Pixar.
- **Gesture is everything.** With no faces, emotion lives in the angle of the head and spine and in the hands.

**For this game:** the game already plans "Faces plain", "figures dark against the light" and "Christian a near-black figure at the gate". Reiniger's rules give a checklist for every new figure, stated in section 5.

### 2.3 Eyvind Earle: *Sleeping Beauty* (1959)

Earle built a unified style from Gothic and pre-Renaissance painting, Persian miniatures, tapestries and Japanese prints [V, [Walt Disney Family Museum](https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/eyvind-earle-artistic-devotion-distinction-sleeping-beauty); [cuckoocomics](https://cuckoocomics.com/eyvind_earle.html)]. He touched nearly every background and spent up to ten days on one [V, [magicnoted](https://magicnoted.substack.com/p/an-ode-to-medieval-and-modern)]. Character designer Marc Davis had to compromise toward his style [V, WDFM blog].

Lessons [M/J]:
- **A consistent shape grammar.** Verticals (Gothic tree trunks, towers), stylised geometric foliage, rhythmic repeats. Every tree in the film is plainly from one hand.
- **Detail as texture, not as information.** Earle's detail is pattern laid flat. It enriches a surface without adding new focal points.
- **The warning.** Earle's backgrounds are so strong that they compete with the characters. Critics of the time and since have said the film is beautiful and cold [M]. That is the trap the owner's rules already avoid: "If you have a few things it begs for more" and "One special thing in the picture at a time."

**For this game:** take the idea that **one hand draws every tree**, not the density. The game's trees are painted pictures, which is fine, but give them one silhouette grammar across levels.

### 2.4 Tyrus Wong: *Bambi* (1942)

Wong's watercolour and pastel sketches, after Song dynasty landscape painting, set the look of *Bambi*. They used minimal brushwork, gentle washes and a few slashes of colour, and they replaced Disney's ornate earlier backgrounds [V, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrus_Wong); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2015/03/28/395328714/the-chinese-paper-son-who-inspired-the-look-of-disneys-bambi); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/how-one-man-drew-1942-bambi-180949466/)].

Lessons [M/J]:
- **Suggestion over description.** A forest is a few dark verticals near, soft lighter verticals far, and a bright opening of light.
- **Detail only where the eye lands.** The rest dissolves. This is "lost and found" applied to a whole picture.
- **Atmosphere as the main subject.** Mist and light, not the trees, carry the mood. This is Song painting's empty space (*liubai*, "leaving white").

**For this game:** Wong is the bridge between the game's layered silhouettes and its watercolour figures. His sketches are exactly "each layer one tone, haze between", with soft edges where the code sometimes has hard ones.

### 2.5 Mary Blair and Claude Coats

**Mary Blair** was colour stylist for *Cinderella*, *Alice* and *Peter Pan*. Her palettes were bold and unexpected, and Marc Davis compared her colour to Matisse [V, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Blair); [D23](https://d23.com/mary-blair/)].

Her lessons are **colour as emotion rather than description**, and **flat shapes in harmonies that would never occur in nature but feel right** [M]. Her concept art was famously stronger than what the films could reproduce [V, [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/arts/10135110/the-woman-behind-the-curtain-mary-blair-at-the-walt-disney-family-museum)].

**For this game:** an occasional unexpected colour key at a story turn is in the spirit of Blair, but it should be rare. Examples: a rose-violet dusk at Palace Beautiful, a sour green at Vanity Fair.

**Claude Coats** was a background painter from *Pinocchio* to *Cinderella* and later an Imagineer [M]. He is known for **dark, rich, theatrically lit interiors** (Geppetto's workshop, Monstro's belly), and later for designing walk-through environments such as the Haunted Mansion, where the visitor's path *is* the staging.

**For this game:** the Interpreter's House is a Coats problem: a series of rooms seen in sequence, each lit as a stage. The game's "one light per room in a great dark" is right.

### 2.6 Studio Ghibli: Kazuo Oga and Nizo Yamamoto

**Kazuo Oga** painted the backgrounds of *My Neighbor Totoro*, *Only Yesterday*, *Princess Mononoke* and others.
- Miyazaki praised his "natural talent for green", and he is credited with the trees' strong presence and the forest's calm [V, secondary blog: [UAL](https://22043013.myblog.arts.ac.uk/?p=48)].
- Miyazaki cared even about the colour of the soil [V, [Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2008-04-30/ghibli-background-artist-kazuo-oga-interviewed)].
- Ghibli's method was to lay the base coat before the paper dried and paint the bulk in one go [V, search summary of Animation Obsessive].

Oga lessons [M/J]:
- **Light is a temperature, not just a brightness.** Sunlit greens go yellow-warm. Shadows go blue-violet but keep their **local colour**. Shadow areas are never dead grey.
- **The sky decides everything.** He often paints the sky first, and every other value is keyed to it.
- **Big clean masses first, a little detail last**, and the detail sits where the light falls.

**Nizo Yamamoto** (*Castle in the Sky*, *Grave of the Fireflies*, *Princess Mononoke*) is known for dramatic cumulus skies and the "Nizo shadow": shadows so blue they glow [M].

**For this game:** "cool shadow, warm light" is already in the game's notes (Level 3's slate path, the rule "decide where the one light is, then make every tone, edge and shadow follow from it"). Oga's version adds **"shadows keep their local colour."** A dark layer should be a darker, cooler version of its own hue, not a mix toward one shared near-black.

### 2.7 Yuri Norstein: *Hedgehog in the Fog* (1975)

Norstein shot cut-outs on several glass planes, about a metre deep with one plane every 25 to 30 cm. The fog was a very thin sheet of paper lowered over the scene and lifted toward the camera frame by frame until everything behind it went soft and white. Dust on the glass thickened the effect [V, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog_in_the_Fog); [Animation Resources](https://animationresources.org/inbetweens-yuri-norsteins-hedgehog-in-the-fog-1975/); sources disagree on details].

Lessons [J]:
- **Fog is a layer between other layers, not over everything.** Its strength per plane falls off with distance from the camera. The hedgehog is clear in front and the horse dissolves behind. That is the game's "Mist goes behind or beside a figure, never between."
- **Fog hides, then reveals.** Shapes (the horse, the owl, the tree) appear out of it one by one, which builds wonder and fear without showing anything frightening. This is the vision's "fear as place, sound and shadow."
- **Imperfection is part of the look.** Paper fibre and dust give the fog body. Pure digital gradients look empty by comparison, which is the game's "plain gradient reads as flat" problem.

**For this game:** Norstein fog suits the Slough, the Valley of the Shadow and the Enchanted Ground. A fog layer should carry grain (soft noise of low amplitude) and be given **per layer**, not as a full-screen wash.

### 2.8 Cartoon Saloon: *The Secret of Kells*, *Song of the Sea*, *Wolfwalkers*

- Adrien Merigeau was background artist on *Kells*, then art director of *Song of the Sea* [V, [Skwigly](https://skwigly.co.uk/tomm-moore); [THR](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com//movie/song-sea/review/732446)].
- An Irish animation director describes the style as flat characters on flat backgrounds, with no formal name for it [V, [Totally Dublin](https://www.totallydublin.ie/?p=30050)].

Lessons [M/J]:
- **Flatness is a decision, not a lack.** *Kells* draws from illuminated manuscripts. Perspective is pushed or dropped, and depth comes from **overlap and value**, not converging lines. This fits the game's "a flat side view with a far horizon, like a long lens" exactly.
- **Two worlds, two drawing languages.** In *Wolfwalkers* (2020) the English town is drawn in hard, straight, woodcut lines and a grid, and the forest in loose, round, pencil-and-watercolour lines. The film's argument is carried by line quality. In *Song of the Sea* the human world and the selkie world differ in curve and colour.
- **Circles and spirals as the shape motif** (Celtic). A film-wide motif ties dozens of locations together.
- **Texture laid over flat shapes** (watercolour grain and paper) unifies hand-drawn and digital work.

**For this game:** the clearest lesson for 17 levels is **one shape grammar for "the way" and another for "the world"**. Bunyan's book is built on that contrast:
- **The way:** straight, narrow, true. "As straight as a rule can make it."
- **The world:** crooked, wide, busy. Vanity Fair, By-path Meadow, the wide ways that "butt down upon" the narrow one.

### 2.9 *The Triplets of Belleville* (2003) and *Klaus* (2019)

**The Triplets of Belleville** (Sylvain Chomet) [M]:
- caricature of *places* as well as people: an impossibly vertical Belleville, a leaning ocean liner;
- sepia, desaturated keys;
- story told with almost no words.

**For this game:** worldly places (Vanity Fair, the town of Fair-speech) may be drawn *exaggerated* in shape, while the way stays plain. Caricature in architecture is a moral comment that a child can read.

**Klaus** (Sergio Pablos) [M; search found nothing usable]:
- 2D characters were lit after animation with volumetric light and texture, so flat drawings sit inside a lit space.
- Its lesson is that **the light on the figure must match the light in the scene**: rim light from the key source, ambient bounce from the ground colour.
- The game has started this (the rim toward the sun in Level 3, `RIM.gold` at the gate, the shared `FIRE` light). It needs one rule across all levels, stated in section 5.

### 2.10 Pixar colour scripts

- Ralph Eggleston made the first colour script, for *Toy Story*, on his own initiative: small pastel drawings of every scene, concentrating on the basic colour. Every Pixar film since has had one [V, [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/audio/playlist/192/2575); [Eggleston interview](https://www.moma.org/interactives/redstudio/interviews/behind/content/eggleston/assets/transcripts.php)].
- Lou Romano's scripts for *The Incredibles* and *Up* give "the emotional tone of each scene, so that you get a sense of not only what the sequences will look like, but what they should feel like" [V, MoMA via search; [/Film](https://www.slashfilm.com/503975/cool-stuff-lou-romanos-color-script-for-pixars-up/)].
- In *Up*, colour comes back into Carl's grey life through the balloons and new companions [V].
- Dice Tsutsumi (*Toy Story 3*, later *The Dam Keeper*) [M]. *The Dam Keeper* is itself a painterly, silhouette-friendly short about a far light and a fog, worth watching for this game.

Principles [M/J]:
1. **A colour script is a strip of thumbnails, one per sequence, read left to right like a line graph of emotion.** Each thumbnail answers four questions: dominant hue, value key (high or low), contrast (hard or soft), and where the brightest spot is.
2. **Colour is held back for the payoff.** If every sequence is saturated, nothing can be. *Up*'s early montage is colourful, the widower's life is grey, and the balloons restore colour.
3. **Repeating colour motifs.** A colour stands for an idea and returns when the idea returns: Ellie's purple, or the hope colour of a film.
4. **Rhythm.** Alternate tension and release (cool and dark, then warm and light) so the eye never tires. Never two climaxes in the same key back to back.

### 2.11 *Spider-Verse* and *Arcane*, briefly

Both show that **painted texture and graphic rules can be applied systematically in code** [M]:
- halftone and hatching in place of soft shading;
- line work driven by rules;
- frame-rate choices (animating "on twos") as style.

For this game the takeaway is narrow but useful. Write the style as **rules a program applies**, not as a pile of one-off fixes. The game already does this with `level(p)`, `GOLD_MAX` and `solidOf`. The edge and value rules in section 5 are of that kind.

## 3. The principles, stated plainly

### 3.1 Value grouping: three or four masses

The oldest rule of landscape and background painting:
- A picture reads at thumbnail size if it is built from **three or four value masses**: for example sky (lightest), far land (light-middle), near land (dark), and the figure accent (darkest, or lightest against a dark).
- Inside each mass, the value range is kept **narrow**. Variations inside a mass must not cross into the next mass's range. That is how detail is added without breaking the read. Painters call this "staying in the mass" [M; standard teaching: Edgar Payne's *Composition of Outdoor Painting*, Hawthorne, notan studies].
- Backgrounds are usually **keyed around the figure's value**, so the character pops. Disney layout and background training puts the most contrast where the character acts [M].
- The game's rule "each depth layer basically one colour" is notan made into code. The risk is **too many layers of nearly equal value**. Seven bands each a step apart read as stripes, and the eye finds no mass. The game has met this ("layers of hills looks too busy", Level 3 build 275).

### 3.2 Shape language

- **Big, middle, small.** Each picture needs one big shape, a few middle shapes and a scatter of small ones, never shapes all of one size. The owner's "four even wavy stripes" problem on the cover (build 279/280) was this.
- **Character by shape:**
  - verticals mean aspiration, stillness, the sacred (Gothic, Friedrich's crosses);
  - horizontals mean rest and peace (Father and Daughter's polder);
  - diagonals mean movement and danger (Martin's overhang);
  - circles mean safety, home, the spiritual (Cartoon Saloon);
  - jagged angles mean threat.
- **Silhouette tangents to avoid:**
  - two edges kissing (a figure's head touching a ridge line);
  - a line running out of a corner;
  - a figure's outline lining up with a background edge.
  
  The game hit one of these when the second hill touched the third on the cover (build 280).

### 3.3 Atmospheric (aerial) perspective

With distance:
- value moves toward the sky's value (lighter by day, toward the fog's value in fog, darker at night under a lit sky);
- contrast falls;
- saturation falls;
- hue shifts toward the sky colour (blue by day, gold at sunset);
- edges soften;
- detail drops away.

The game does the value part. To make it complete, the code should also apply **softening of edges and loss of detail with distance**. Distant ridges in a layered silhouette should have smoother outlines, fewer small notches and a slightly feathered top edge, and near layers crisper. Earle and Oga both let far trees become shapes without internal marks.

### 3.4 Colour temperature

- **One key light sets the temperature.** Surfaces facing it take its colour. Shadows take the colour of the *fill*, usually the sky (cool by day) or bounce from the ground (warm under a sunset, green in a forest).
- Warm against cool is the strongest contrast after value. Use it for the focal point.
- **Never mix temperatures without a reason.** Two warm lights in one picture fight. The owner's "one light at a time" rule is right, and it is the Rembrandt rule.
- **Hue shift through a gradient.** Real skies do not go from one colour to another in a straight line. They pass through a third (blue, to a pale green-grey, to gold). Background painters exaggerate that middle band slightly to make skies glow [M/J].

### 3.5 Edge control: lost and found

- **Found (hard) edges** draw the eye. **Lost (soft or vanishing) edges** let shapes merge and rest.
- Rules painters follow [M, standard teaching]:
  1. the hardest edge and the highest contrast at the focal point;
  2. edges soften with distance;
  3. edges soften where values are close (shadow into shadow);
  4. a turning form has a softer edge than a cut form;
  5. **lose part of every long edge.** An unbroken edge along its whole length looks pasted on.
- Wong, Norstein and Dudok de Wit all lose most edges and find a few. The figure's head and the light are found; the ground into fog is lost.
- The game's own lessons already point here: "a fade that stops at .85 leaves ghosts", "a blend must never run along one straight level line", and the crisp-edge mountain of build 192.

### 3.6 Staging a small figure

1. **Contrast.** The figure is the darkest dark against the lightest light, or the reverse. Put the brightest part of the background directly behind or next to the figure (Friedrich's *Monk by the Sea* and *Wanderer*; Father and Daughter's figure against a pale sky).
2. **Isolation.** A clear halo of empty space, or of a single value, around the figure. No busy texture touches the silhouette.
3. **Leading lines.** Road, ridge and light beams point at the figure or at the goal it walks toward.
4. **Scale as meaning.** A small figure under a great sky reads as humility or awe. A figure filling the frame reads as intimacy. Cut between them on purpose. Bunyan's turning points (Cross, Apollyon, the River) want the small figure.
5. **The walk direction is story.** Left to right is "going forward" for Western readers. A figure walking left is going back, which Level 1's "out, then back" already uses. Facing away from us (Friedrich's *Rückenfigur*) invites the viewer to stand in his place.
6. **Horizon placement decides mood.** A low horizon gives sky, air, hope (the Cross, Beulah). A high horizon gives earth, weight, oppression (the Slough, Doubting Castle, the Valley).

### 3.7 How a colour script carries emotion across a feature

Seen across *Up*, *Bambi*, *The Red Turtle* and *Wolfwalkers* [M/J]:
- **A home key** opens the film, and its return closes it, changed.
- **A descent** follows: the key darkens and desaturates as trouble comes.
- **A false light** comes in the middle: a seductive warm key at the wrong place (*Pinocchio*'s Pleasure Island). Bunyan has several: Vanity Fair, By-path Meadow, the Flatterer.
- **The low point** is the darkest, often monochrome key.
- **The payoff** is the first full colour, saved for the emotional climax.
- **Motif colours** recur: one hue stands for hope and appears in small doses all through, so the climax can flood with it.

### 3.8 Cyclical and journey films

- **Journey films** (*Bambi* across seasons, *The Red Turtle* across a life, *Kells*, *Father and Daughter*) hold one-ness through **constants**: the same horizon height, the same few motifs, the same figure scale and the same light source. They express progress through **one changing parameter**: season, age or time of day.
- Father and Daughter changes the season and the figure's age and keeps the frame. Bambi changes the season and keeps the forest. Bunyan changes the *place* and keeps the light and the burden. The game must decide which parameter drives the arc. My recommendation: **time of day and weather**, which the notes have already begun (rain to gold, Level 4's night, Level 5's first dawn).

## 4. Recommendations: 17 levels as ONE film

### 4.1 Write a one-page colour script now, before Level 6 [J]

Make a strip of 17 small thumbnails, one per level, ideally drawn in code at 96 x 54 so the program itself can render it from each level's settings. Each thumbnail states five things:
- (a) time of day and weather;
- (b) the value key: high (light), middle, or low (dark);
- (c) the dominant hue and the accent hue;
- (d) where the far light is and how strong (`HV.LEVEL` already does this);
- (e) horizon height.

A starting proposal, to argue with:

Rules the strip should obey:
- **Never two neighbouring levels in the same key.**
- **Save full saturated gold for 5 and 17**, with 14 as a foretaste. Everywhere else gold appears only in the far light itself. This is the Pixar balloon rule, and it matches the vision's point that "the Cross's dawn is paler" than Level 3's start today.
- **The false-light levels (3's opening, 11, 15) get warm or gaudy colour that is never the far light's exact gold.** Children learn the colour of the true light by its rarity.

### 4.2 Constants that make 17 levels one film [J]

Lock these into code as shared values, the way `HV` and `STEP_VOL` are shared:

1. **The far light.** One function (`HV.draw`), one gold, strength only rising. Already begun. Extend it to every level as planned.
2. **The figure scale.** Christian is the same size on screen in every walking stretch. The game already has the rule "never shrink the person the child steers". Wide shots are moments, not walks.
3. **The horizon grammar.** A fixed small set of horizon heights (for example low .25, middle .45, high .65 of the picture) and nothing between. Changing between them is then a story event the eye feels.
4. **The layer count.** Three to five depth layers per scene, never more (see 3.1). Name them: sky, far, middle, near, ground. Each scene's palette is a list of five colours.
5. **One shape grammar for the way.** The road is always one clean horizontal band, flat and straight, from Level 1 to the River. The crooked ways (By-path Meadow, the turning at the hill, the wide ways at the gate) are always drawn **crooked and wider**, the *Wolfwalkers* principle. This gives the book's moral a visual language a seven-year-old reads without words.
6. **One tree grammar.** Plain English trees (oak, ash, elm) drawn by one hand, silhouette first. Special trees only once.
7. **One texture.** A shared paper grain over everything (very faint, the same everywhere) unifies code-drawn scenery, painted pictures and video figures, as Cartoon Saloon lays its watercolour grain over everything. It also fixes the "too clean gradient" look. **Caution:** the owner disliked busy small detail in Level 2, so keep it below notice. It must be felt, not seen.
8. **Recurring transitions.** The same kind of dissolve between levels (a fade through the next level's sky colour, or through mist) so the film breathes the same way each time.

### 4.3 Father and Daughter as the model [J]

- **The dyke moment.** Give each level one fixed frame where Christian stops and the far light is seen: the same composition (low horizon, him small at the left third, the light ahead at the right third). It is the game's refrain. Over 17 levels the child sees the same picture change: rain, mist, a burning hill, night, dawn, the Valley's black, Vanity's glare, the Delectable Mountains, the River. It costs almost nothing. It is a camera position and a composition rule, and it gives the whole game the cumulative force of Father and Daughter's returning dyke.
- **Long cast shadows** on flat ground at low sun (the game has begun, `SUNSH` in Level 3). Use them wherever there is a low key light. They are the cheapest way to show the light's direction and the ground's flatness.
- **Weather as one wash.** Rain, snow and fog each as one full-frame quality, not as decoration. Level 1's rain and Level 2's mist already do this.
- **Silence and the walk cycle as music.** The notes' rule, "silence and the world's own sounds are the ground; music is punctuation", is exactly Father and Daughter's discipline, except that its waltz runs throughout. Keep the walk rhythm steady and calm.
- **Leave out more.** When a scene feels empty, first try a stronger sky before adding a prop.

### 4.4 Lotte Reiniger as the model for figures [J]

- **The silhouette test.** Every figure pose must read when filled pure black at game size:
  - hands clear of the body where the gesture matters (Evangelist pointing, Help reaching, Good-will pulling);
  - head angle legible;
  - the burden always a separate bump on the outline.
  
  Run `solidOf` in black and look before accepting any new figure.
- **Lace against mass** for special objects. The cross stays plain, as the owner chose. But the Interpreter's house lattice, the iron cage, Vanity Fair's booths and Doubting Castle's gate can be filigree silhouettes, which code does well and cheaply.
- **The figures are the darkest values; the backgrounds never reach black** except where the story needs a void (the Valley, the dungeon). Then the figure needs a rim of light to separate it. Reiniger kept her backgrounds in graded translucent grey for this reason.
- **Tint by level**, as she tinted by act. The game's `drawFrame` tint is already this. Make the tint come from the colour script table.

### 4.5 Edge and value rules the code can follow [J]

Rules written so a program can apply them (`d` is a layer's depth from 0 near to 1 far, as `level(p)` uses):

1. **Value step between layers.** Adjacent depth layers differ by at least 8% lightness and at most 20%. Fewer than 8% reads as one mass (use one layer); more than 20% reads as a stripe. Total range far to near is set by the level's key: high key 60 to 85% lightness, low key 5 to 35%.
2. **Contrast falls with distance.** Within a layer, internal variation is at most `(1 - d) * 6%` lightness. Far layers are nearly flat.
3. **Edge softness rises with distance.** A layer's top edge is blurred by about `0.5 + 3d` pixels at 1x. Bake it once by drawing small and stretching, never with `ctx.filter`.
4. **Outline detail falls with distance.** The amplitude of small notches on a ridge outline is about `(1 - d)^1.5` of the near layer's. Far ridges are smooth. This is what makes "realistic ridgelines" read as distant.
5. **Lose part of every long edge.** Any silhouette edge longer than about a third of the screen fades into the haze or the next layer for at least 10% of its length. This applies to the wall of Salvation, the road's far edge and long ridges.
6. **The figure's ground.** Behind the figure (about two figure-widths either side), the background's value differs from the figure's by at least 35% lightness. If not, raise the haze behind him locally, as Level 3's mist bank does in build 314. Run this check in the stills test: sample the background and the figure, and warn when they are too close.
7. **The hardest edge is at the focal point.** Only the figure and the far light (and the act target: the hand, the door, the roll) get pixel-sharp edges. Everything else gets at least a half-pixel soft edge.
8. **Shadows are cool and keep local colour.** A layer's shadow side is its own hue, 15 to 25% darker, shifted toward the sky's hue, never toward neutral grey. Light from the key source is warm and shifts toward the light's hue.
9. **Fog per layer.** The fog strength over layer `d` is `fogLevel * d^γ` (γ about 1.5), so near layers stay clear. Never lay a full-screen fog over a figure (the existing rule). Add faint grain to fog (Norstein).
10. **One accent of saturated colour per screen, at most.** Everything else stays within the level's narrow palette. The red of the turtle, or of the burden's ropes, is spent once.
11. **Thumbnail test.** Every new scene must read when the stills tool renders it at 96 x 54 and blurred: three or four masses, the figure findable, the light findable. Add this as an option to `tools/stills*.js` so it is a habit, like the twice-size close-up.

### 4.6 Two cautions

- **The style must stay quiet.** Earle shows that a strong background style can overpower the story, and the owner's taste (simplicity, restraint, "awe first") already leans away from that. Apply these rules to remove more than to add.
- **The owner decides.** Every recommendation above is a proposal. The colour script table especially is a sketch to argue over with the owner, level by level.
