# 04. Russian and Eastern European art as a source

One of twelve art-school deep dives (vision chat, after build 591). **Ideas only: nothing in the game was changed and nothing was spent.** Every idea that is not in Bunyan is a departure and needs the owner's yes. Settled rulings in `CLAUDE.md` stand over anything here (the one far light, layers of one tone, plain faces, Amazing Grace saved, Level 5's hill settled, smoke soft and never crisp).

## Why this tradition fits this game

Russian art of the icon and of the late nineteenth century "mood landscape" is the closest thing in European painting to what this game already is: **a pilgrim walking a road under a sky that means something.** Three strands matter.

1. **The icon** treats light not as weather but as the presence of God: the gold ground is not a sky, it is the light the scene happens *in*. That is the game's far light, raised to a principle.
2. **The Wanderers' landscapes** (Levitan, Kuindzhi, Shishkin, Nesterov) made the empty road, the plain and the low horizon into the subject itself, and made light the drama. That is the game's side-scrolling road.
3. **Soviet-era animation** (Norstein, Petrov) solved, by hand, the exact technical problem the game solves in code: depth by stacked flat layers and fog between them. Norstein's *Hedgehog in the Fog* is the nearest artistic cousin of the game's look that exists.

Behind all three stands the Russian **strannik**, the wandering pilgrim: the man of *The Way of a Pilgrim* (19th c.) who walks across Russia with a knapsack holding a Bible, the *Philokalia* (in its Slavonic version, the *Dobrotolubiye*) and dried bread, saying the Jesus Prayer, "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me", without ceasing **[V]**. He is Christian's cousin: a man with a book and a burden, on foot, who meets elders on the road. Nesterov painted his kind; Levitan painted his road.

## 1. Icon painting

### The Ladder of Divine Ascent (Sinai, late 12th century)

The most useful single picture in this report for a pilgrimage game. **[V]** A ladder of **thirty rungs** (one for each chapter of St John Climacus's book) runs **diagonally from the lower left to the upper right**, splitting the panel into an earthly and a heavenly half. Monks climb it; at the top Christ receives St John Climacus. **Small black winged demons** fly at the climbers and pull them off with hands, hooks and arrows; some monks cling, some fall, one is swallowed by the open jaws of a dragon at the foot. Angels watch from the upper left and a group of monks prays from the lower right. All on a gold ground.

Principles to take:
- **The whole journey in one diagonal.** Left to right, low to high: the same direction as the game's road. The ladder is a timeline you can see entire.
- **The enemy is small and the way is large.** The demons are little, quick, many; the danger is in their *number and nearness*, not their size. This is the game's own rule, "the place frightens and the creature is hardly seen", found in a 12th-century masterpiece.
- **Falling is shown, but the eye goes up.** Christ's outstretched hand at the top is where the composition ends.
- **Help is from the side.** The praying monks on the ground and the angels are not on the ladder; they help from outside it. That is Help in the Slough and Evangelist under the hill.

### Rublev's Trinity (c. 1411-1425) and reverse perspective

Rublev's three angels at Abraham's table sit inside a circle; the footstools and the table are drawn in **reverse perspective** (their edges widen *away* from us), so the space opens toward the viewer instead of closing toward a vanishing point **[M]**. Pavel Florensky's essay *Reverse Perspective* (1919) argued that this is a deliberate refusal of the Renaissance single fixed eye, and that the icon is "a border between two worlds" **[V]**. The gold, in Byzantine thought, is linked to the **uncreated light** of Gregory Palamas, the light seen on Mount Tabor **[V]** (the link is from theology, not a Florensky quotation).

For the game: the game already refuses deep perspective ("flat, not deep", builds 40 to 43). The icon gives that refusal a *meaning*: flatness is not a limitation, it is the way sacred pictures have always looked. Level 3's gate in true perspective (builds 455 to 469) is the exception; elsewhere the flat side view is in good company.

Colour: Rublev's Trinity is famous for its **one blue** (the "Rublev blue", *golubets*) among soft ochres and gold **[M]**. One cool note in a warm field is exactly the colour rule Level 3 found for itself ("warm light against one cool shade", build 224).

### Theophanes the Greek (Feofan Grek)

The Transfiguration icon from Pereslavl-Zalessky (late 14th or early 15th century; the date is disputed in the sources) **[V]** shows Christ in a mandorla of light whose rays strike the three apostles to the ground. Theophanes painted light as **white strokes laid straight onto dark faces** ("highlights" called *probela*), not modelled form **[M]**. The light is a thing that *arrives*, in rays, and the human figures are thrown down by it.

For the game: the Shining Ones at the Cross are already "diffuse human shapes of light" (build 403). Theophanes suggests the next step, if ever wanted: the light is drawn as a few hard bright strokes on the figure's edge, not as a glow. The game's `RIM` edge light is this idea, already.

### Last Judgement icons

Russian Last Judgement icons (the Novgorod type, 15th century) run a **river of fire** from Christ's feet down into hell and, famously, a **serpent** coiling up the centre of the panel from hell toward the judgement, its body ringed with the names of sins **[V]** (the serpent in the Novgorod icon at the Tretyakov is the subject of a 2007 article by Priscilla Hunt). The composition is layered in horizontal **registers**, heaven at the top, earth in the middle, hell at the foot.

For the game: horizontal registers are the game's layers. The darker rooms (room 7, the dream of judgement) already show the trumpet and the man on the cloud only as light on a wall, which is gentler than any icon and right for seven-year-olds. Keep it so; the icons are a source for *structure*, not for imagery of hell.

## 2. Arkhip Kuindzhi: light that seems to shine out of the canvas

*Moonlit Night on the Dnieper* (1880) was shown **alone, in a darkened room, the windows curtained, lit by one electric lamp aimed at it** **[V]**. Viewers reportedly looked behind the canvas for a hidden light **[V]** (anecdotal). The original has since **darkened badly** because of Kuindzhi's unstable paint mixtures **[V]**.

His tricks, as far as they can be checked:
- **One light, everything else dark.** The moon and its path on the river are the only bright things; the land is a near-black mass with almost no detail **[M]**.
- **Warm darks against cool lights.** Reddish earth tones in the shadows, cool silver-green in the light, so the light reads as colder and brighter by contrast **[V]** (from a gallery page; weaker evidence).
- **Small dark strokes inside the highlight** that make it shimmer **[V]** (same source).
- **Staging.** The room was part of the picture. A Cambridge *Slavic Review* article calls this an "intermedial environment" **[V]**.

What it means for a game drawn in code on a phone: **the phone is Kuindzhi's dark room.** The screen is self-lit; surrounding a small bright area with deep near-black makes it read as *emitting* light, not as a pale patch. The game's far light (`heaven.js`, `HV.draw`) already avoids a disc and edge. Kuindzhi adds two cheap rules:
1. **Darken everything away from the light more than feels safe** when the light matters (Level 1's first touch on the light; the Cross at dawn). The light needs no extra brightness; its surroundings need less.
2. **A cool light needs warm darks.** Where the far light is silver (`REACH` in `heaven.js`), let the nearest dark layer lean warm.

His *Red Sunset*, *Rainbow* and *Birch Grove* use the same method in day: a band of glowing light caught between two dark masses (a dark foreground and a dark sky). *Birch Grove* (1879) lays **flat, sunlit patches on the grass between dark trunks**, simplified almost to a poster **[M]**: a model for the garden inside the wicket-gate and for any "sunlit clearing".

## 3. Isaac Levitan: the road as subject, the mood landscape

### *Vladimirka* (1892)

An empty dirt road across a vast flat plain, running from the foreground to the horizon's blue haze under a grey sky **[V]**. It is the **Vladimir Highway, the road along which convicts in chains were marched to Siberia** **[V]**; Levitan, coming upon it while out hunting, remembered that, and painted **the road without the convicts** **[V]**. A tiny figure of a woman pilgrim and a roadside shrine stand near the right **[M]** (not confirmed in any source this session).

Principles:
- **The emptiness carries the story.** Nothing happens; the history is in the road. This is the strongest argument in all of Russian art for the game's "about two minutes of speech a level" rule: the walk between words is not dead time if the land carries meaning.
- **Low horizon, big sky, one road.** Exactly Level 5's and Level 2's composition.
- **The road's worn tracks are drawn as a few soft parallel bands**, not as detail. That is the gravel way of Level 3 (builds 334 to 340).

### *Above Eternal Peace* (1894)

From a high bluff, a tiny wooden church and a graveyard of leaning crosses look out over a huge grey lake and a storm sky **[V]** for the date and setting; Levitan called it his most personal work **[M]**. The scale is the point: a small human thing at the edge of an enormous, indifferent, beautiful world; the church's one lit window is the only warmth **[M]**.

For the game: this is **Level 14 (the Delectable Mountains, the shepherds' look out)** and also **Level 16 (the Enchanted Ground)**, almost composed already. One lit window in a great grey: the game's own Rembrandt rule, with Levitan's cold palette.

### The "mood landscape" (*peizazh nastroeniya*)

Levitan's term, roughly: the landscape carries a state of soul rather than a view **[M]**. Grey thaw, autumn, evening; muted colour, no strong contrasts, a few horizontal bands. This is close to what the owner asked of every level ("simple, natural, calm, not cartoonish"). It is also a warning: Levitan's quietness only works because **one element is placed exactly** (a road, a church, a raft). Quiet without a single placed thing is just dull. That is the game's own rule, "one special thing in the picture at a time" (build 40).

## 4. Shishkin, Aivazovsky, Vrubel: what to take and what to leave

- **Ivan Shishkin**, *Rye* (1878): a golden field with a road through it and a few tall pines; the trees give the scale **[V]** for the painting's basic facts. Shishkin is a **detail realist** (every pine needle) and the game should *not* copy his detail. Take his composition: tall dark verticals at uneven spacing over a pale field. Level 1's wheat with its few groves (build 508) is already Shishkin's structure.
- **Ivan Aivazovsky**, *The Ninth Wave* (1850): shipwrecked men on a spar at sunrise, the sun burning through the spray of a huge wave **[V]**. The light **through** the water (translucent green at the wave's crest) is his famous effect **[M]**. Useful only for **Level 17, the River**: the light seen *through* the water as Christian goes under, the sun of the City burning through spray. Leave the melodrama.
- **Mikhail Vrubel**, *The Demon Seated* (1890): a brooding figure built of **crystalline facets**, like a mosaic of broken colour **[M]**. Too dark and too adult for the game's people. But the faceting is a way to draw **rock that is not a texture**: Apollyon's valley floor and Doubting Castle's walls could be faceted planes of a few close tones, a code-friendly kind of rock that is not photographic (the owner turned photographic rock and crisp smoke down more than once).

## 5. Viktor Vasnetsov: *Knight at the Crossroads* (1878; large version 1882)

A knight on a white horse stops at dusk before a **stone with an inscription** in the empty steppe; skulls and bones lie in the grass; a black raven sits on the stone **[V]**. The inscription (in its folk-tale form) warns that **"if you go straight, you will not live; there is no way for one walking, riding or flying"**; the crossroads stone with three fates is a recurring motif in Russian tales **[V]**.

This is **the choice of ways** made into one picture, and *The Pilgrim's Progress* is full of them: Worldly Wiseman's way round the hill, the crooked wide ways that "butt down upon" the narrow way (`{67}`), the hill Difficulty with Danger and Destruction to either side, By-path Meadow, the way that "seemed as straight" with the Flatterer.

Principles:
- **The choice is a still moment.** The horse has stopped; the knight's head is bowed toward the stone. Choices in this game already halt the walk; Vasnetsov says: let the picture *hold its breath* there (light falls, wind drops).
- **The warning is written on the land itself.** Not a talking character; a stone.
- **The horizon is empty in every direction.** All roads look the same; only the words differ.

For the game: **Level 6, Hill Difficulty** (`{100}`ff.): the three ways at the hill's foot, Difficulty straight up, Danger and Destruction round each side. A low carved stone at the fork (like the sign over the wicket-gate in Level 3, touchable and opened close up with `KS.look`), reading with Bunyan's own names, would be a quotation of Vasnetsov in the game's own language. No skulls; perhaps one crow.

## 6. Mikhail Nesterov: pilgrims and wanderers in a thin, holy light

### *The Vision to the Youth Bartholomew* (1889-1890)

The boy who will become St Sergius of Radonezh, sent to find lost horses, meets a mysterious old monk under an oak in a field; the land behind is a **pale autumn northern landscape**, thin birches, a wooden church, wide low hills, painted in **muted, silvery, almost colourless tones**, and the old monk's halo is a thin gold ring **[M]** (dates and title [V]). The holy figure is half absorbed into the landscape: a dark shape against the tree, his face hidden in his hood **[M]**.

Principles:
- **Holiness is quiet.** No glow, no rays: a dark figure, a thin ring of gold, a quiet land. This is the strongest model in the report for **Evangelist and the Interpreter**, and for the Shining Ones' first appearance before they become light.
- **Thin trees, wide land, pale sky** as a *spiritual* palette. Nesterov's birches are the vertical counterweight to Levitan's horizontal plain.
- **A hooded figure whose face is not shown** is the game's plain-face rule already.

### *Holy Rus'* (1901-1905, Russian Museum)

Pilgrims and wanderers, the sick seeking healing, a child, an old woman, stand in a snowy northern landscape before Christ and three saints coming toward them out of a monastery **[V]** for the subject and dates; the critic Voloshin mocked the Christ as a "mannequin in a spectacular pose", and Tolstoy answered that Christ had to be painted as the people before him see him **[V]**. The lesson cuts both ways: **the pilgrims are the painting's strength, the divine figure its weakness.** The game's rule, already agreed, that the holiest things are shown as light and not as figures (the Shining Ones, the man upon the cloud in room 7), is the right side of that argument.

## 7. Nicholas Roerich: flat colour, spiritual mountains

Roerich's early *Guests from Overseas* (1901) shows Viking ships with **striped sails and dragon prows in flat, bright, decorative colour** on a blue sea **[V]** for the title and date. His later Himalayan paintings (1920s-40s) are mountains in **flat tempera bands of one colour each**, violet, blue, rose, gold, with sharp clean edges and almost no modelling **[M]**. Roerich died in 1947; his works are public domain in the EU since 2018 and pre-1929 works are public domain in the US **[M]** (check each file).

Principles:
- **Each mountain is one flat tone, with a hard clean edge.** This *is* the game's layered look, in colour. Roerich proves that one-tone layers can be ecstatic, not only melancholy.
- **Unexpected colour in the high places.** Violet snow, rose rock, green sky. He uses colour as the sign that a place is holy.

For the game: **Level 14 (the Delectable Mountains)** and **Level 17 (the land of Beulah)**. The owner wants "GRIS"-like colour returning as the story heals; Roerich is the painter of exactly that colour. After the Cross the palette can open, mountain by mountain, toward Roerich's violets and roses, still one flat tone a layer.

## 8. Ivan Bilibin: the book as an object

Bilibin's illustrated Russian fairy tales (about 1899-1902, *Vasilisa the Beautiful* and others) are **flat ink-outlined pictures in watercolour framed by ornamental borders** drawn from folk embroidery, carved wood and old manuscripts; the borders carry small scenes and motifs from the tale **[V]** for the books and dates; the description of style **[M]**. His three horsemen of *Vasilisa*, the White (day), the Red (sun) and the Black (night), are one of the great images of time passing in a story **[M]**.

For the game, the most direct use in this report: the **title page, the contents and the end cards**. The book on the title is already a 1678 printed book (the owner's ruling). An **end card per level framed by a narrow painted border** whose small motifs are *that level's things* (Level 1: the roll and the wicket-gate's light; Level 2: the oak and the steps; Level 3: the hill's smoke and the gate; Level 5: the cross and the sealed roll) would make each finished level feel like a page earned. Keep the border in the game's own tones (no folk colours), the way the owner asked the cover to be "more like story itself" (build 276). Bilibin's Black Horseman (night) could also inspire a quiet transition card: a single figure crossing the frame as one level turns into night and the next.

## 9. Polish and Czech painters

- **Jacek Malczewski**, *Melancholia* (1890-1894): a crowd of figures pours out of the painter's studio in one long diagonal wave, toward an open window **[V]** for the title, date and setting. A whole people's history as **one procession across the picture**. Useful for **Level 11, Vanity Fair** (the crowd as one wave, one tone) and for the memories on the slope in Level 5 (the relived past as a procession).
- **Józef Chełmoński**: Polish plains, storks, cranes, *Indian Summer*, *Dawn: the Kingdom of Birds* (1906) **[V]** for the titles. Low horizons, huge skies, **birds as the soul of the land**. Birds already cross Level 3's opening; Chełmoński says a flight of cranes, high and far, can carry the whole feeling of a place at dawn. A skein of birds crossing the sky at the Cross after the burden falls, heading toward the far light, would be a cheap, wordless image of release (a departure).
- **Alphonse Mucha**, *The Slav Epic* (1910-1926, twenty canvases about 6 x 8 m, tempera) **[V]** for the scale and dates. Mucha painted history in **huge pale, dreamy fields with a few figures picked out**, the past floating as translucent ghosts in the sky **[M]**. Mucha died in 1939 (public domain in most places); no Commons file was confirmed this session. His **pale figures floating in the upper sky** are a precedent for the game's memories in Level 5's sky (builds 444 to 466) and for anything seen in a vision.

## 10. Animation: Norstein and Petrov

### Yuri Norstein, *Hedgehog in the Fog* (1975) and *Tale of Tales* (1979)

The closest technical ancestor of the game. Norstein built his **own multiplane stand: several sheets of glass a few inches apart, a camera above** **[V]**; later films used **ten layers or more** **[V]**. Figures are **cut-out paper puppets** moved a fraction at a time on their own glass **[V]**. **The fog is a sheet of thin tracing paper** placed between layers: laid directly over the hedgehog it is nearly invisible, moved several layers toward the camera he vanishes **[V]**. One source says the glass was left dusty to thicken the haze **[V]** (single source).

What the game can take, almost literally:
1. **Fog has a depth, not a strength.** In Norstein, how much a thing is hidden depends on *how many fog sheets lie between it and the eye*. In code that is: each depth layer drawn, then one translucent haze band, then the next layer. The game's levels already do this with one haze per layer; the lesson is that **moving a figure *through* the layers** (stepping one layer back into the fog) is a storytelling tool. Help steps out of the mist in Level 2 exactly this way (build 388). Christian walking into the Valley of the Shadow (Level 9) should step *back* layer by layer, not just be darkened.
2. **The hedgehog's white horse.** In *Hedgehog in the Fog*, a white horse appears in the fog as a dim, huge, beautiful shape, half there **[M]**. The fear and the wonder are the same image. This is the awe-before-fright rule in one shot. For Level 9, the Valley: the voices and shapes in the dark should be half-seen *beautiful* shapes, never monsters.
3. **Cut-outs that move a little.** Norstein's figures barely move; a head turns, a paw lifts. The game's breathing in code was disliked in the house (build 302) but the near-still figure is right.
4. **The Wolf's lamp in *Tale of Tales*** (the little grey wolf carrying a baby into the forest, a lit house in the dark) **[M]**: a single warm window in a black night with snow falling. The Interpreter's house by night (`bakeDoorScene`) is that picture.

### Alexander Petrov, *The Old Man and the Sea* (1999)

**Paint-on-glass**: slow-drying oil paint pushed with the **fingertips** on several sheets of glass at different heights (for depth), each frame painted over the last under the camera **[V]**; more than **29,000 images**, the first large-format (IMAX) film to win the Academy Award for animated short **[V]** (the length of production differs in sources). The look is **light dissolving forms**: one image melts into the next, a dream becomes the sea.

For the game: Petrov is the master of the **transition**. Where the game fades through black between rooms or levels, Petrov **paints one scene into the next**: the shape of the last scene becomes the first shape of the new one. A cheap code version: the last picture's main silhouette (the oak, the hill, the cross) is kept as a dark mask and the next scene is revealed *inside* and then *around* it. This fits Level 5's memories laid on the slope (build 589), which are already Petrov-like.

## 11. Tarkovsky

*Andrei Rublev* (1966) is in black and white for three hours and turns to **colour only at the end, on Rublev's icons** **[M]**. *Stalker* (1979) is a pilgrimage: three men walk through "the Zone" toward a Room said to grant wishes; the journey is slow, wet, overgrown, the way never straight, the guide throwing nuts tied to cloth ahead to test the path **[M]**.

Principles (neither film is free to use; these are ideas only):
- **Colour arrives at the holy thing.** The game already does this at the Cross (`gold`, the sun, colour coming back). Tarkovsky is the authority for keeping the colour *withheld* until then: Level 5 should stay close to grey until the burden falls.
- **Water and slowness.** Tarkovsky's long held shots of water over stones. The Slough's mirror strips and the River (Level 17) would gain from one long still moment, no words, only water.
- **The guide who tests the way ahead.** The Stalker's thrown nut is the Shepherds' "perspective glass", the Evangelist pointing, Help's steps found by lights. The lights finding the stones in Level 2 are already a Tarkovsky image.

## 12. Principles mapped to the game's layered code look

| Principle | Source | In the game's language |
|---|---|---|
| Light is a presence, not weather | gold ground, Theophanes | the far light (`HV`) never a disc; it is what the scene happens in |
| A self-lit picture glows when its surroundings sink | Kuindzhi's dark room | at key light moments, darken away from the light rather than brighten it |
| Cool light, warm darks | Kuindzhi | silver `REACH` light against warm near-black layers |
| One cool note in a warm field | Rublev's blue | Level 3's slate path; one cool layer per level |
| Flatness is sacred, not naive | reverse perspective, Florensky | keep the flat side view; perspective only as an exception |
| The empty road carries the meaning | Levitan, *Vladimirka* | let walks be wordless; give the land one placed thing |
| One small human thing in a vast quiet | Levitan, *Above Eternal Peace* | a single lit window, chapel, or cross in a grey world |
| The journey seen whole on a diagonal | the Ladder icon | a wide look that shows the whole way left-low to right-high |
| The enemy small and many, the way large | the Ladder icon | demons, doubts, arrows as small quick shapes, never a big monster |
| A choice is a held breath before a stone | Vasnetsov | choices as still moments with a carved, touchable sign |
| Holiness is quiet | Nesterov | the holy figures dark and still, a thin gold edge at most |
| Each mountain one flat colour | Roerich | after the Cross, colour returns layer by layer in flat tones |
| The book as an object with borders | Bilibin | end cards framed by small motifs of that level |
| Fog has depth | Norstein | haze between every layer; figures step back into it |
| One scene paints into the next | Petrov | transitions through a kept silhouette, not through black |
| Colour withheld until the holy thing | Tarkovsky | keep Level 5 grey until the burden falls |

## 13. Concrete ideas, level by level

Each is a departure from the game as built and needs the owner's yes; none needs money.

1. **City of Destruction.** Kuindzhi's dark room at the first touch on the light: as Evangelist says "Do you see that shining light?", sink the rain and the field a step darker around the light for a few seconds (no brighter light). Levitan's *Vladimirka* in the run's last stretch through the fields (build 494): once the houses are gone, let the road and sky be empty for a breath before Obstinate and Pliable come.
2. **Slough of Despond.** Norstein's fog: Pliable leaving into the mist could step back *through* two haze layers (smaller and paler as he goes) instead of fading. Tarkovsky: one long wordless moment of still water under the oak after Help's "Go on your way".
3. **Worldly Wiseman and the hill.** Vasnetsov at the crossing where the oak stands: the place where the ways cross could have a low worn stone, so the choice to turn aside has a sign on the land (Bunyan has no stone here: a departure; perhaps better saved for Level 6). The Ladder icon's small quick shapes: the stones rolling down the rocky path are already this.
4. **Wicket-Gate and Interpreter's House.** Norstein's *Tale of Tales* lit house: the Interpreter's house by night is already that picture; keep its one lit window as the only warmth. Room 5 (the palace): the armed men as the Ladder's small dark shapes, many and close, the brave man cutting through them up and right like a climber on the ladder.
5. **The Cross.** Tarkovsky and Kuindzhi: keep the colour withheld and the hill near-black until the burden falls, then let the light come as the only bright thing (already the plan). Chełmoński: after the burden falls, a high far skein of birds crossing toward the sun. Nesterov: the Shining Ones' *first* moment could be three dark still shapes with a thin bright edge, becoming light only as they speak.
6. **Hill Difficulty, the lost roll, the lions.** **Vasnetsov's stone** at the foot: three ways, a carved stone naming Difficulty, Danger and Destruction (`{100}`ff.), touchable, opened close up like the wicket-gate's sign. The arbour as a Levitan *Above Eternal Peace* in summer: a small shelter on a high slope over a huge view.
7. **Palace Beautiful.** Bilibin's book: the room where he is shown "the records of the greatest antiquity" (`{125}` region, check the book) as painted manuscript pages with borders. The chamber called Peace, facing the sunrising: one window, Kuindzhi's dawn.
8. **Apollyon.** The Ladder's rule, small enemy, large way, reversed only once: Apollyon must be big, so take Vrubel's *faceting* for him, a shape of broken dark planes, never a face; and Aivazovsky's light *through* spray for the "fiery darts" (seen as light, not as fire).
9. **Valley of the Shadow of Death.** **Norstein's fog layers** as the whole level: the path narrow between a ditch and a quag (`{2xx}`, check the book), Christian stepping back into the fog layer by layer, shapes half-seen and beautiful like the white horse. The voice that whispers blasphemies as a sound with no source.
10. **Faithful (and Talkative).** Nesterov's two wanderers on a wide pale land: two small dark figures walking together on a Levitan road.
11. **Vanity Fair.** Malczewski's procession: the crowd as one diagonal wave in one tone. Roerich's flat bright colour, used *falsely* here only (a fair's paint, gaudy, flat), so that true colour later reads differently. Never the far light here (the awe critic's rule).
12. **Hopeful, By-ends, the silver mine.** Kuindzhi's glow used as a *lure*: the mine Demas guards could shine Kuindzhi-bright in a dark ravine, the false light; the true far light stays soft and far.
13. **By-path Meadow and Doubting Castle.** Vasnetsov's stone again, but for the *wrong* way: the stile into the meadow as the crossroads moment, held. Doubting Castle in Vrubel's faceted dark stone; the key called Promise as the one gold thing.
14. **The Delectable Mountains.** **Roerich**: mountains in flat bands of violet, blue and rose, the first full colour since the Cross. Levitan's *Above Eternal Peace* for the hill called Clear, where the shepherds lend the perspective glass: from a high bluff, the far gate glimpsed over a vast grey plain.
15. **Ignorance, the Flatterer's net.** The Ladder's falling monk: the net as small dark lines thrown from the side, the Shining One with a whip of small cords seen only as light. Ignorance's end kept quiet (the ruling), as small as a monk falling at the ladder's edge.
16. **The Enchanted Ground.** Levitan's mood landscape at its sleepiest: grey, warm, still air, almost no contrast, the far light dim through haze. The danger is only drowsiness: Norstein's fog thickening slowly, layer by layer.
17. **Beulah, the River, the Celestial City.** **The gold ground at last.** Through the whole game the far light has been a light in a sky; at the City it becomes the ground of the picture itself, the icon's gold, everything else laid on it in thin flat layers (reverse of the whole game's dark). Aivazovsky's light through water as Christian goes under the River; Petrov's painting of one scene into the next as the River becomes the City; Mucha's pale floating figures for the hosts that meet them. Roerich's colour for Beulah.
