## 0. Why look here at all

The game's settled look is atmospheric layered silhouettes, each depth layer one tone, pale far and dark near, one far light as the goal, few things, "not video-game looking, not cartoonish" (CLAUDE.md, Art direction). The masters named so far are painters: John Martin, Friedrich, Rembrandt, Turner, Ansel Adams, Hasui and Yoshida, with *Journey*, *Inside/Limbo* and *GRIS* among games.

Comics and sci-fi/fantasy illustration give the game three things painting cannot:

1. **Sequence.** A painter makes one picture. A cartoonist makes a journey out of many pictures, and has to decide what changes and what stays the same from one to the next. A side-scroller is one long sequential picture, so this is the same job.
2. **Graphic economy.** The comics tradition has a craft vocabulary for exactly what the game already does: *spotting blacks* (choosing where the solid black masses go), *clear line* (an even outline with flat fills), *silhouette readability*, and *negative space*. The words are useful in the notes.
3. **Scale and the quiet apocalypse.** Sci-fi illustration has spent fifty years drawing a tiny figure in front of something vast, and the last fifteen drawing ruins that are calm, overgrown and hopeful rather than violent. That is the vocabulary of the owner's "year 2200" idea (`concept-2200.html`: four directions, *A Fallen towers*, *B Rust and ash*, *C The green return*, *D Glass age*, each with a cold sky turning to a warm one and lit windows).

The section headings below follow those three: the journey, the graphic language, scale, then the 2200 direction, then concrete recommendations and the taste-picker list.

## 1. The journey told in sequence

### Jirō Taniguchi, *The Walking Man* (1990–91) [V]
A middle-aged man walks his own neighbourhood. There is very little dialogue and no plot; an episode is climbing a tree barefoot, or standing under a tap on a hot day. Reviewers compare it to Ozu's films. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Walking_Man_(manga)); [ANN, House of 1000 Manga](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/house-of-1000-manga/2011-12-29))

**What it teaches this game.** *The Walking Man* proves that a walk is enough, if the walker notices. Taniguchi's method [M]: long sequences of near-identical panels where only the figure's place moves, broken by one panel that opens wide onto what he sees (a sky, a tree, a bird). The world is drawn with architectural patience; the man is small, plain and calm. That is precisely the game's "walk, a story moment, walk", and the owner's rule that nothing moves on its own. Its lesson for the empty stretches between story moments: they are not dead time to be shortened, they are *noticing* time; put one small thing to notice in each (the game already does this with touchables: birds, chimney smoke, lizards, the oak's bird). Taniguchi would say: fewer of them, and each given its own beat.

### Shaun Tan, *The Arrival* (2006) [V]
A 128-page wordless graphic novel of an immigrant's journey to a strange city, drawn in pencil to look like old sepia photographs; Tan cites Raymond Briggs's *The Snowman* as an influence; Angoulême prize 2008. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Arrival_(graphic_novel)); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2011/09/23/140119806/in-wordless-imagery-an-immigrants-timeless-tale))

**What it teaches.** (1) *Grid as breath:* Tan uses pages of small equal panels (a clock, a face, a hand) for waiting and leaving, and a full-page spread for arrival in the vast city [M]. The game's equivalent is the camera: close and still for the waiting, the wide pull-back for the arrival (already done at Level 3's foot and Level 5's wide shots). (2) *One tone for a whole book:* sepia carries the memory feeling across 128 pages without monotony because the *values* range from paper-white to deep brown. This supports Level 3's monochrome opening (build 399) and suggests the 2200 version could be one tone per level, not one palette for all. (3) *Strangeness made gentle:* the creatures and machines are odd but never threatening; the fear is the immigrant's, not the world's. That is the right register for children and for 2200.

### Moebius (Jean Giraud): *Arzach*, *The Airtight Garage*, *The Incal* [V/M]
*Arzach* (Métal Hurlant, 1975) is four wordless stories of a silent rider on a white pterodactyl crossing a desolate land ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arzach)). [M] Moebius drew deserts as wide white planes with a hairline horizon, mesas and strange spires drawn in a fine even line with hatching only in the shadows, and colours laid flat in pale, sunlit pastels (rose, sand, a thin turquoise sky). *The Incal* is literally a pilgrimage: a small, frightened, ordinary man (John Difool) is carried from a vast vertical city down to the light, through tests, toward a mystical union; it ends where it began, a cycle.

**What it teaches.** (1) *Light as emptiness:* Moebius's desert light is made by leaving the page bare; heat and silence are white space. The game's gold endings could use more empty pale sky and fewer things. (2) *Clean line over a quiet wash:* his figures are simple outlines; the landscape does the feeling. (3) *A silent hero:* Arzach never speaks and the reader fills him in. Christian's plain face is the same choice. Moebius is the strongest single reference for a 2200 "Glass age" or desert direction: wide pale air, one strange monument, a small rider.

### Hugo Pratt, *Corto Maltese* [M]
Pratt's blacks are poured, not drawn: a figure is often a few brushed masses with the white paper doing the light. His horizons are low and his seas and skies huge. The lesson is *how little* is needed for a figure to read: a hat, a coat, a stance.

### Jean-Pierre Gibrat (*Le Sursis*, *Le Vol du corbeau*) [M]
Watercolour over line in muted greens and ochres, rain and wet streets. He shows how watercolour comics hold a quiet melancholy without going grey: one warm note (a red scarf, a lit window) in each muted page. That is Level 1's lit doorways.

### Craig Thompson, *Blankets* (2003) and *Habibi* (2011) [V/M]
*Blankets* is an autobiography of growing up in an evangelical family in rural Wisconsin and his strained relationship with that faith ([WPR](https://www.wpr.org/agriculture/graphic-novelist-craig-thompsons-ginseng-roots-start-his-central-wisconsin-childhood); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blankets_(comics))). [M] Drawn in flowing brush, black and white; snow is the white of the page; spiritual moments become ornament, pattern and floating light. *Habibi* fills pages with Arabic calligraphy and border ornament.

**What it teaches, with care.** Thompson is the one artist here who draws faith from the inside, and the reader will know he left it; the owner should know that before borrowing from him. What carries over cleanly: (1) *snow and silence as white page:* the Cross's dawn could be a moment where most of the picture simply goes pale; (2) *the spiritual as ornament and light, not as figures:* the Shining Ones as diffuse light (build 403) are already in this spirit; (3) his page-turning devices (a blanket's pattern flowing across panels) suggest that a pattern can carry the Bible's motif through the levels (the keepsakes' red ribbon could reappear as a thread in the scenery).

### Chris Ware (book design) [M]
Ware's books treat the whole object (cover, endpapers, diagrams, cutaways of houses) as one designed thing. Relevant to the game's book-cover title page and the Interpreter's House cutaway (build 393): Ware's house cross-sections are flat, frontal, evenly lit, with tiny figures in each room, read like a map. The game's cutaway is already Ware-like; the lesson is to keep it strictly frontal and diagrammatic, never in perspective.

### How comics show a journey: the rules distilled
- **Direction is meaning.** In left-to-right reading cultures, moving right is going forward, going left is going back or home. The game already uses this (Level 1 "out, then back", the run back right). Keep "toward the City" always rightward; any leftward walk should feel like a return or a mistake (Level 3's run back).
- **The establishing shot then the close.** Every arrival in a comic gets one wide panel; every emotion one close panel. The game's camera has learned this level by level; write it as a rule.
- **The repeated frame.** Taniguchi and Tan repeat a composition with one change (the light lower, the figure further). Reusing a framing across levels (Christian small at the left third, the far light at the right third) would make the levels rhyme.
- **Gutters are time.** What happens between panels the reader imagines. The game's fades and cuts (Level 3's cut to black over the brow, the mist "in process of time") are gutters; they let the child fill in, which is the comics' gentlest power.

## 2. The graphic language: silhouette, spotting blacks, line versus mass

### Mike Mignola (*Hellboy*) [M]
Mignola composes in big flat black shapes first, then a few lines inside the light areas; whole panels are mostly black with a figure's edge picked out. He is famous for "spotting blacks": deciding where the solid black masses fall to lead the eye. His quiet "pillow" panels (a statue, a moon, a bird on a branch) between action panels slow the reader down.
**Lesson:** the game's nearest layer is already near-black; Mignola would make the *black shapes* themselves designed (a deliberate arch of branches, a doorway) rather than incidental. His insert panels are a model for the game's moments of rest: a short still shot of one object (the oak, the lantern, the burden in the tomb) between story beats. **Limit:** his subjects are demons; take the method, not the mood.

### Sergio Toppi [V/M]
Dense India-ink cross-hatching balanced against large untouched white areas; the negative space isolates a face or gives a landscape its scale and leads the eye to the climax ([Sessions College](https://www.sessions.edu/notes-on-design/why-they-work-sergio-toppi/); [Paul Gravett](https://paulgravett.com/articles/article/sergio_toppi)). Toppi often abandons the panel grid: a single huge figure fills the page and the story moves around it.
**Lesson:** detail is allowed, *if it is pooled in one place and surrounded by emptiness*. That matches the owner's "one special thing in the picture at a time".

### Dino Battaglia and Alberto Breccia [M]
Battaglia (Italian adaptations of Poe, Melville, Lovecraft) drew in grey washes, scraped and blotted, with figures half-dissolved in fog and white light. Breccia (Argentine, *Mort Cinder*, his Lovecraft) used collage, monotype, razor scratches on black: texture standing for the unspeakable. **Lesson:** Battaglia is the closest comics cousin to the game's mist and watercolour; his figures *dissolve* into the atmosphere at their edges instead of being outlined. This supports soft, lost edges in the far layers (the Level 3 critic's "lost and found edges" in the mannequin study). Breccia is a limit: too harsh for 7-to-10, but his texture-for-dread idea suits the Valley of the Shadow if only a little is taken.

### Jeff Smith, *Bone* [M]
A long fantasy journey for children (and adults), drawn in a lively brush line; the cartoon "Bones" walk through a realistically drawn valley. The two registers sit together because the landscape is serious. **Lesson:** the owner has already ruled "not cartoonish", but *Bone* shows that children accept a serious, beautiful landscape completely; the game need not soften the scenery for its audience, only the events.

### Tony Millionaire, Nick Hayes [M]
Millionaire (*Maakies*, *Sock Monkey*) draws in an antique engraved hatching, as if Victorian book illustration; Hayes (*The Rime of the Modern Mariner*, *Woody Guthrie*) draws in a linocut-like line, continuous and rhythmic. Both show that a comic can look *old* without pastiche. **Lesson:** the game's book chrome (title page, keepsakes, the stone over the gate) already speaks 17th-century print; these artists show how far an old-print look can go in the scenes themselves (hatching as the only shading). A possible style for a special memory sequence, not for the whole game.

### Line versus mass: where this game stands
Comics divide roughly into *line* traditions (Hergé's clear line, Moebius, Taniguchi) and *mass* traditions (Pratt, Mignola, Battaglia). The game is firmly *mass*: no outlines, value layers, one tone each. That is right for atmosphere and for small phone screens (silhouettes read at 220 px tall; fine lines do not). Where line can help: **only at the light**. Moebius, Mignola and Pratt all use a thin lit edge where light meets a dark form (the game's "silver lining" and gold rims). The owner has tried and pulled these back several times (builds 177, 217, 282, 304, 467): the comics' answer is that a rim should be *one* thin line on *one* edge of *one* thing in the picture, the focus; not on every figure all the time.

## 3. Vast scale: how sci-fi and fantasy artists make the world huge

### Tsutomu Nihei, *Blame!* (1997–2003) [M]
A lone wanderer climbs through an endless megastructure, kilometres of concrete and pipe, for ten volumes with almost no dialogue. Scale is made by: (1) the figure a few pixels tall on a double-page spread; (2) repetition of an architectural unit receding into haze (the eye counts and gives up); (3) atmospheric perspective even indoors; (4) no horizon, so no end. **Lesson:** repeat-and-recede is the cheapest scale trick in code: a row of identical arches, pillars or cliff spurs, each paler and smaller. **Limit:** *Blame!* is violent and lonely without hope; take only the architecture.

### Syd Mead, John Harris, Chris Foss, Ralph McQuarrie, Roger Dean [M]
- **John Harris** paints colossal structures and ships dissolved in glowing atmosphere, with tiny crowds at the bottom edge. The structure is never fully seen; light eats its far end. *This is the John Martin method in sci-fi dress* and the closest match to Level 3's mountain.
- **Ralph McQuarrie** (Star Wars concept art) painted plain, low-horizon landscapes, two suns, a figure and a far shape, in muted gouache: simplicity as epic. His Tatooine is a Friedrich.
- **Syd Mead** paints clean futures at golden hour, wet reflective ground, warm and hopeful; the source for a *Glass age* 2200 direction, but his precision is the opposite of the game's softness.
- **Chris Foss** paints giant striped ships over small landscapes: too busy and colourful here.
- **Roger Dean** (Yes album covers): floating islands, organic arches, roots that become bridges. Lesson for the *Green return*: nature growing over structure in long curving lines.

### Alan Lee and John Howe (Tolkien) [M]
The most relevant fantasy illustrators of a *journey*. Lee works in pale pencil and watercolour, mist, small figures on long roads, ruined towers half-lost in hills; Howe in stronger ink and colour, figures under towering architecture. Lee is almost the game's existing look in a book: travellers seen from behind or side-on, the road leading in, the destination in haze. **Lesson:** Lee draws ruins as *landscape*, worn down to hills; architecture returning to nature, never smoking wreckage. That is the right model for 2200's fallen towers.

### Gustave Doré (comics' ancestor) [V]
Doré's Dante, Bible, Milton and Coleridge plates (1860s–70s) are the ancestor of both comics and fantasy illustration: wood engravings built in tone, not line, tiny figures before vast rock, ice or light. Three plates are almost game scenes:
- *Paradiso, Canto 31*: the Empyrean as a great rose of light, the travellers tiny at its edge. This is the far light made a place: the Celestial City (Level 17).
- *Purgatorio, "The Burden of Pride"*: souls bent under huge stones on their backs on a mountain terrace. A literal picture of the burden; a direct parallel to Christian.
- *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, "The ice was all around"*: a tiny ship in a cathedral of ice; scale by surrounding.
Doré also shows the danger: his hells are crowded with bodies. Take his *heavens and journeys*, not his punishments.

### Golden Age illustrators: Kay Nielsen, Edmund Dulac, Arthur Rackham, Sidney Sime, John Bauer [V]
- **Kay Nielsen** (*East of the Sun and West of the Moon*, 1914): tall narrow compositions, a tiny figure on a vast stylised plain or sea, sweeping curves of wind and hair, a far castle on the horizon. *Soria Moria Castle* is exactly a lone traveller seeing his far goal glowing on the horizon. Nielsen is the best answer to "beauty first": decorative, elegant, every curve designed, yet huge in feeling. He later worked on Disney's *Fantasia* "Night on Bald Mountain" and "Ave Maria" [M], the latter a procession of lights through a forest of arches: almost a Pilgrim's Progress sequence.
- **Edmund Dulac:** jewel-like night blues and golds, Persian miniature space. Good for one rich moment (Beulah, the Celestial City), too ornate for the road.
- **Arthur Rackham:** gnarled trees with faces, sepia and muted wash, pen outline. His trees are the danger the game already avoids ("no exotic ones", no cartoons): his twisted roots are good for the Valley of the Shadow but the faces are not.
- **Sidney Sime** (Dunsany): huge dark masses, minute figures, black-and-white mystery. *Idle Days on the Yann* river plates are dream journeys by water: a reference for the River (Level 17).
- **John Bauer** (Swedish): tiny figures in immense, dark, vertical moss forests, muted greens and gold; a model for any forest stretch and very gentle in feeling.

### The scale rules distilled (all agree)
1. A vast thing is never shown whole; an edge leaves the picture or haze eats its far end.
2. The person stays a constant small size; the world grows (the owner's own rule from build 181).
3. Repetition receding into haze reads as infinite.
4. Detail on the vast thing must be small (the owner's scale rule from build 153: "anything drawn ON a great thing is a measure of it").
5. A low horizon gives sky and awe; a high horizon gives oppression. Choose per level.

## 4. The quiet apocalypse, and how it keeps hope (for the 2200 direction)

### Simon Stålenhag: *Tales from the Loop*, *The Electric State* [V/M]
*The Electric State* is a road journey: a girl and a small yellow robot cross a ruined alternate America in 1997, past wrecked giant drones ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electric_State)). Stålenhag based it on a happy three-week road trip with his family ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/cool-tech/behind-the-unsettling-sci-fi-landscapes-of-simon-stalenhags-electric-state)). [M] *Tales from the Loop* shows 1980s Swedish countryside with huge machines and cooling towers quietly decaying in the fields while children play around them.
**How he paints it:** ordinary weather (snow, overcast, low sun), realistic flat landscape, the monumental ruin in the middle distance, children or a small figure in the foreground going about life. The machine is never attacking; it is *part of the landscape*, rusting, grown over. **The tone:** melancholy, not terror. For *Electric State* the tone is bleak (it is about addiction and loss); for *Loop* it is wistful and warm. **Lesson for 2200:** *Loop* is the model, *Electric State* the journey shape. Take: the ruin as landscape, everyday weather, the child's small purposeful figure. Avoid: his photographic realism (too detailed for the layered silhouette look) and *Electric State*'s despair.

### Hayao Miyazaki, *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* (manga, 1982–94) [M]
A thousand years after the Seven Days of Fire, a poisonous forest (the Sea of Corruption) covers the world. The great reveal: the forest is *cleaning* the poisoned soil, and beneath it lie pure sand and clean water. Drawn in sepia pencil with dense, small panels. **This is the hope structure that fits Bunyan:** what looks like the ruin is the healing. The Slough of Despond is described by Bunyan as the place where the scum of conviction gathers, and the King's labourers have worked to mend it for sixteen hundred years (Level 2's history, build 417): Miyazaki's labourers and Bunyan's are the same image. For 2200's *Green return*: the green that grows over the towers is grace, not decay.

### Enki Bilal, Zdzisław Beksiński: the limits [M]
- **Bilal** (*Nikopol*): ruined grey-blue cities, painted faces with blood, oppressive politics. Beautiful cold palette (worth a look for direction A, *Fallen towers*), but the mood is hopeless and adult.
- **Beksiński**: rotting cathedrals, skeletal figures in sulfur light, astonishing technique, deeply disturbing. **Too dark for 7-to-10, without exception.** He is useful only as a line not to cross: if a 2200 study starts to resemble Beksiński (bone, rot, flayed architecture), it has gone wrong.

### How the gentle post-apocalypse keeps hope (from Stålenhag's *Loop*, *Nausicaä*, Lee's ruins, and games such as *Journey*)
1. **No bodies, no enemy.** The catastrophe is long past; we see its scars, not its event.
2. **Nature returning** (moss, flowers, trees in the cracks, birds).
3. **Small human warmth**: one lit window, a cooking fire, a figure walking with purpose. (`concept-2200.html` already gives every direction a lit-window colour `win`.)
4. **Weather turning**: a cold sky warming toward the goal; exactly the game's rain-to-gold.
5. **The ruin is old and quiet**: worn, rounded, softened by haze; never sharp, smoking or fresh.
6. **The light still there**: the far light as the one thing unchanged by the catastrophe. In 2200 the far light should look *exactly* as in the 17th-century game; it is the bridge between the two versions.

### The four 2200 directions read through these artists
- **A · Fallen towers** (cool blue-grey): Lee's ruins-as-hills plus Harris's dissolving megastructure. Danger: Bilal's chill. Keep towers leaning, broken off, wrapped in haze; never twisted rebar detail.
- **B · Rust and ash** (warm brown-grey): Stålenhag's rusting machines in fields, Moebius's desert. Danger: reads as war. Keep the ash as soft dust under a gold sky.
- **C · The green return** (green-grey): *Nausicaä*'s healing forest, Roger Dean's grown bridges, Bauer's moss. **Best fit for the book's meaning** (wilderness and grace) and for children. My recommendation.
- **D · Glass age** (violet, cyan windows): Mead and McQuarrie, Moebius's pale sci-fi spires. Most unusual; risks looking like a different game and like "video game sci-fi" (the cyan `win`). Use it for one level (Vanity Fair as a glittering glass city) rather than as the whole look.

## 5. Recommendations for this game (concrete)

**For the game as it is:**
1. **Name the methods in the notes.** Add to "Picture rules": *spot the blacks* (place the near-black masses on purpose to lead the eye to the one special thing) and *one lit edge per picture* (Mignola/Pratt: one rim on the focus only), which resolves the repeated back-and-forth over figure rims.
2. **A "pillow shot" between beats** (Mignola, Taniguchi): after a big moment, a two-second still of one object with no figure (the burden at the tomb's mouth, the lantern, the oak, the gate shut). Free; uses only camera moves on existing scenes.
3. **Rhyming framings across levels** (Tan, Taniguchi): pick two compositions and reuse them in every level: (a) the *arrival frame* (Christian small, lower-left third, the far light upper-right third, empty middle), (b) the *threshold frame* (a dark near shape framing an opening onto light: Level 1's gate, Level 3's wicket-gate, the Interpreter's door, Palace Beautiful). A child will feel the pattern.
4. **Repeat-and-recede for scale** (Nihei, Harris): for the Valley of the Shadow and the Delectable Mountains, rows of identical rock spurs or pillars each paler and smaller. Cheap in code (Level 3's `crag()` already does half of this).
5. **Doré's "Burden of Pride"** as a reference for Level 6 (Hill Difficulty): figures bent under burdens on a terrace; and his *Paradiso 31* for Level 17's Celestial City (the far light finally becomes a place, a vast rose of light with the travellers small at its rim).
6. **Nielsen's *Soria Moria Castle*** as the reference for the Delectable Mountains' first sight of the City through the shepherds' glass (Level 14): a tiny figure on a long ridge, the far castle shining on the horizon.
7. **Bauer's forests** for any wooded stretch (the arbour on Hill Difficulty), Sime's Yann plates for the River.
8. **Memory sequences in an "old print" register** (Millionaire, Hayes, Thompson): if flashbacks return anywhere, render them in a different medium (engraving-like hatching or one-colour wash) so they read as memory, as Tan uses sepia.
9. **Do not take:** Rackham's tree faces, Doré's crowded hells, Beksiński, Bilal, Breccia's harshness, Foss's colour, Mead's precision, *Blame!*'s violence.

**For the 2200 direction:**
1. **Choose C, The green return**, with Miyazaki's meaning: the growth over the ruins is the healing. Keep the book's places as ruins worn into landscape: the City of Destruction as fallen towers at the start (a cool blue A palette for Level 1 only), greening level by level, gold at the City. The direction then *progresses* through A → C → gold, which is the game's existing weather arc.
2. **The far light never changes** between 1678 and 2200; it is the thread of the whole idea.
3. **Christian's clothes** stay "worn, handmade, layered cloth" (the art rule already allows "a post-apocalyptic version may come"): a hood and a pack, perhaps one salvaged object (a lantern made of old glass). No goggles or gadgets, as the rules already say.
4. **Ruins as the far and middle layers only**; the near layer stays earth, grass, road. A child walks on a path, and the ruins are the hills.
5. **Vanity Fair as the one Glass-age level** (D): glittering, cyan-lit, busy, false light; the only place the 2200 future looks shiny, which makes the point.
6. **Test with one still first** (the owner's habit): the Level 2 oak and slough redrawn with a half-sunken tower in the far mist (Stålenhag's machine-in-the-field) costs nothing in code.
