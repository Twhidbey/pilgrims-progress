# The art of Bunyan's own day (c. 1640 to 1700): what a Bedfordshire tinker saw, and what the game can take from it

Art deep dive, report 5 of 12. Topic: not pictures OF the book (that is `vision/1-how-artists-drew-it.md`) but the pictures, prints, places, maps and things that surrounded John Bunyan (1628 to 1688) and his first readers. Nothing in the game was changed; nothing was spent.

**How far to trust it.** Web searches worked; most pages could not be opened from this session (Gutenberg, archive.org, the Hockliffe project, many museum sites do not resolve here). Wikimedia Commons' API did answer, so **every image in the taste-picker list at the end was checked to exist on `upload.wikimedia.org`, with its licence, through the Commons API** on the date of writing. Marks: **[V]** checked in a search result or the Commons API; **[M]** from memory, well known but not re-checked here; **[U]** uncertain or disputed. Every idea that is not in Bunyan is a departure and needs the owner's yes.

## 1. The short version

Bunyan lived in the one generation when two opposite ways of seeing met in England:

1. **The Puritan's word-first, picture-shy way.** Images in churches had been smashed within his boyhood (William Dowsing's commission of December 1643 named "Images & pictures of Saints & supersticious inscriptions"; Dowsing visited over 250 churches in Cambridgeshire and Suffolk in 1643 to 1644 [V]). The image a Puritan allowed was the **emblem**: a small plain picture held on a leash by a motto and a verse, so the picture only ever *means* something. Bunyan defends his whole book this way: "I have used similitudes" (Hosea 12:10, printed on his own title page, and on the game's title page). **The Interpreter's House is an emblem book walked through** (a picture, a question, the meaning).
2. **The Dutch way of seeing land.** Across the North Sea, in exactly these years, painters invented the modern landscape: low horizons, skies taking two thirds or more of the picture, flat land in bands of light and shadow, and (from about 1626 to 1650) a **tonal** manner in which a whole picture is nearly one colour, brown-grey-green, wet air between every layer (van Goyen, Salomon van Ruysdael) [V]. This is, almost word for word, the game's own settled look ("each depth layer basically one colour, overlapping with haze, pale far layers, darker near layers").

Between those two sit **the cheap prints ordinary people actually owned**: broadside ballads and chapbooks with crude, endlessly re-used woodcuts [V], plague broadsides with a skeleton under "Lord have mercy upon us" [V]; and the most game-like object of the century, **John Ogilby's *Britannia* (1675)**: 100 copper plates of England's roads drawn as **scroll strips**, the road a straight band running up each strip, every church, windmill, bridge, hill and turning drawn small along it, at one inch to the mile [V]. It came out the same years Bunyan was writing his road book. A side-scrolling road with landmarks along it is a 1675 idea.

The game already lives in this world without knowing it. This report names the ancestors, so the next levels can borrow on purpose.

## 2. What an Englishman of Bunyan's class actually saw

**His place.** Elstow, a mile south of Bedford, in the flat clay vale of the Great Ouse [M]. Its landmarks, which Bunyan saw every day as a boy:
- **Elstow Abbey church**, all that is left of a nunnery church begun in 1078, with a **detached bell tower** standing apart from the church (one of two in Bedfordshire) [V]. Bunyan was christened there in 1628 [V]; in *Grace Abounding* he tells of ringing its bells and of his fear, after he gave it up, that a bell or the tower itself would fall on him [M]. Local tradition makes this tower the model for **Beelzebub's castle** shooting arrows at the gate [V, as a tradition reported by Britain Express]. Note for Level 3/4: a plain square bell tower standing *apart* from the building beside it is an authentic Bunyan silhouette, and quieter than the epic castle now in the game.
- **The Moot Hall** on Elstow green: a two-storey timber-framed market hall with red-brick infill, dated variously 1440-50 or about 1500, now a museum of 17th-century life [V]. Bunyan played tip-cat on that green on the Sunday he heard a voice from heaven (*Grace Abounding*) [M]. This is the face of the "town" a child should see in Level 1: jettied timber frames, not stone.
- **Bedford**: the Ouse, its medieval bridge, St Paul's church, the County Gaol at the corner of High Street and Silver Street, where Bunyan lay from 1660 to 1672 and again in 1676/77 [V]. The famous story that he wrote *The Pilgrim's Progress* in the little town gaol **on Bedford Bridge** is a tradition most historians now doubt [V: Bedfordshire Archives; marked U]. Either way: **the book was dreamed in a "den"**, a small stone room, and Robert White's 1679 frontispiece shows Bunyan asleep over that den [V, in report 1].
- **Further off, the Greensand Ridge**: the first real hill south of Bedford. Local tradition says the steep road up to Ampthill is the **Hill Difficulty**, and **Houghton House** (built c. 1615 to 1621, a ruin since 1797, English Heritage) is "reputed to have been the model for the Palace Beautiful" [V, as tradition]. So two of the book's places may be a day's walk from Bunyan's door: **a flat wet vale (the Slough's country), then a sandy hill, a fine house on its brow.** That is a real geography the game can honour in Levels 6 and 7.

**What it looked like on the ground** [M unless marked]:
- **Buildings**: timber frame with wattle-and-daub or brick panels, steep thatch (in Bedfordshire, straw), small casements with leaded diamond panes, brick chimneys added to older halls, a few stone churches; brick spreading after 1666. Barns with great doors; **post mills** (a wooden windmill turned on one post) on every rise of the flat country. Not stone cottages under slate: that is northern or western.
- **The road**: unmetalled, rutted, deep mud in winter (Ogilby's age measured roads; it did not pave them), with fords, causeways, stone bridges, finger-posts rare, a gibbet at a crossroads, inns. "Slough" was an ordinary word for a mire on a road.
- **Fields**: still largely open-field strips round Bedford villages, hedged closes nearer the houses; wheat, barley, beans; commons with cattle and geese.
- **People** (Bunyan's class: a tinker, then a preacher): plain wool doublet and breeches or a leather jerkin, linen shirt, a broad felt hat, worsted stockings, heavy shoes; women in a waistcoat or bodice, petticoat, linen coif under a hat, an apron; colours from cheap dyes (browns, dun, grey, a dull green, faded red); "russet" was the old name for coarse country cloth, fading from use by mid-century [V, costume historian via search]. A plain wool doublet and breeches of 1625 to 1635 survives at the V&A as a rare example of everyday men's dress [V]. The game's rule "worn, handmade, layered cloth; no lace, ruffs, buckles, neat buttons" is right for Bunyan's class: the lace, ruffs and bright silks are the **court** (Lely's portraits), which in Bunyan's book belongs to **Vanity Fair** and to By-ends' relations.

**What he saw in pictures.** Very little that was "art". In a cottage: perhaps a ballad pasted on the wall, an almanac, a Bible (maybe with a woodcut title page), a chapbook. In church after 1643: whitewashed walls, the royal arms, painted texts (the Commandments, the Lord's Prayer) on boards. In a gentleman's house: portraits, maps, perhaps Dutch pictures. So the visual culture of the book's first readers is **a printed one: black lines on cream paper, a picture with words under it.** The game's title page (the 1678 title page, IM Fell type, laid paper) has already got this right.

## 3. The emblem: the picture that must mean

**What an emblem is** [M, standard]: three parts. A *motto* (inscriptio) above; a *picture* (pictura) in a round or oval frame; a *verse* (subscriptio) below that turns the picture into a lesson. The reader looks, is puzzled for a moment, reads, and understands. It was the Protestant's licensed image: not to be worshipped, only to be read.

**The three English books to know:**
- **Francis Quarles, *Emblemes* (1635)**: the most popular English book of verse of the century [M]. Its plates, engraved by William Marshall and William Simpson, are mostly copied from Herman Hugo's Jesuit *Pia Desideria* (Antwerp 1624, designs by Boetius à Bolswert) [V]. Their hero is **Anima, the Soul, drawn as a small child**, beside **Divine Love, another child**, in little scenes: the soul in a cage, the soul weighed in scales, the soul lost in a maze while Love guides it by a thread, the soul looking at the world through a telescope, the soul bound to a skeleton [M]. A child-sized pilgrim among large symbolic objects, in a round frame: **this is the nearest 17th-century thing to the game's own figure**. The maze-with-a-guide image is Bunyan's whole plot in one picture.
- **George Wither, *A Collection of Emblemes, Ancient and Moderne* (1635)**: 200 round copper engravings by Crispijn de Passe, first made for Rollenhagen's book; with a spinning-wheel "lottery" at the back that sends the reader to a random emblem [M]. Every picture is a circle; every one is a landscape with one object in the foreground that carries the meaning (a candle, an anchor, a snail, a winged globe).
- **John Bunyan, *A Book for Boys and Girls, or Country Rhimes for Children* (1686)** [V]: the first notable English book of verse for children [V]; 74 poems [V] on country things (clouds, birds, a top, chalk, a candle, the sun's reflection on the clouds, moles, the cuckoo, the butterfly, the sacraments) [V]. It had **no pictures** in 1686 [M; later recast as *Divine Emblems*, 1701, with small woodcuts, V]. Bunyan's pattern [M]: a verse describing the thing ("Upon the Lark and the Fowler", "Upon the Whipping of a Top", "Upon a Snail", "Of the Boy and Butterfly", "Upon a Candle" [V for the candle]), then a second part headed **"Comparison"** that says what it means. **Bunyan wrote emblems for children of 7 to 10 himself.** That is the strongest licence this game could have.

**How the book is built of emblems.** The Interpreter's rooms (the dusty parlour, Passion and Patience, the fire fed with oil, the man in the iron cage) are emblems staged as rooms: Christian asks "What means this?", the Interpreter gives the "Comparison". Many readers have noted this [M]. Outside the house, the book keeps doing it: the burden, the roll, the key called Promise, the river, the glass on the Delectable Mountains, Lot's wife as a pillar. Bunyan thinks in **objects that carry meanings**.

**Aesthetic principle: one object, one meaning, framed, with a word under it.** It matches three of the owner's rules already: "one special thing in the picture at a time", "stripped down ... when in doubt take one away", and "the words are a thin strip of light text on the dark road under everyone's feet" (that strip *is* a subscriptio).

## 4. Popular print: ballads, chapbooks, plague sheets

- **Broadside ballads** (one sheet, a song, a tune name, one to four woodcuts): Pepys collected over 1,800 [V]. Woodcuts were **stock blocks**, passed between printers and reused "again and again", some for more than a century, worm-holed and cracked [V: EBBA]; one stock figure is on more than a hundred ballads [V]. The look: thick black lines, solid black grounds, figures side-on, a strip of ground, a sun with a face in the corner.
- **Chapbooks** (small cheap booklets sold by pedlars): the same blocks; *The Pilgrim's Progress* itself became a chapbook favourite in the 18th century [V, report 1].
- **The plague of 1665 and the Fire of 1666**: London's weekly *Bills of Mortality*, the red cross and "Lord have mercy upon us" on infected doors [V], John Dunstall's broadsheet of nine scenes (flight by boat, carts of dead, return) [V]; Hollar's maps and views of London before and after the Fire [V]. Bunyan's first readers in 1678 had lived through both. **The "City of Destruction" was not abstract to them.** A fire seen across the water at night (Hollar's view, Dutch paintings of the Fire) is the most real image of "this our city will be burned with fire from heaven" that the century made.

**Principle: re-use is the tradition, not a cheat.** The game's practice of reusing Level 1's plain field trees, Help's walk as a ghost, and Christian's walk on the cover is exactly how 17th-century printers worked. The one thing they did not do was hide it; neither should the game worry about it, so long as the owner's rule holds (special trees are never reused).

## 5. Dutch landscape: the game's real school

Bunyan probably never saw these pictures, but his Dutch contemporaries were inventing, in the same decades, the very look the owner chose. Each painter gives one principle.

| Painter | Key work | Principle for the game |
|---|---|---|
| **Jan van Goyen** (1596-1656), and **Salomon van Ruysdael** | river and dune views of the 1630s-40s, e.g. *View of Dordrecht* | **The tonal phase.** A limited range of yellow, brown and pale green [V, Princeton], earth colours with moist air [V, Thyssen], a low viewpoint and one strong diagonal [V]. A whole picture in one key. This is the colour model for any level that must feel grey and wet (Slough, Enchanted Ground). |
| **Jacob van Ruisdael** (c.1629-1682) | *The Jewish Cemetery* (c.1654-55, two versions, Dresden and Detroit); *View of Haarlem with Bleaching Fields*; *Wheat Fields* | **The moral landscape.** The cemetery is an allegory: tombs, a ruined church, a dead tree, a torrent, a storm passing and a rainbow (transience and hope) [M]. The *Haarlempjes*: flat land in bands of sun and cloud shadow under a vast sky, a church far off. *Wheat Fields*: a sandy road through wheat to a low horizon under huge clouds, tiny walkers on it (a road!). |
| **Meindert Hobbema** (1638-1709) | *The Avenue at Middelharnis* (1689, National Gallery, London) | A road ruled **dead straight** to a central vanishing point between two rows of lopped poplars, a tiny man walking up it, low horizon, sky two thirds. **Good-will's narrow way "as straight as a rule can make it" ({66}) in one picture, painted in Bunyan's lifetime.** It is a perspective view, against the game's flat side view, so use it as one held still, not as a level. |
| **Aelbert Cuyp** (1620-1691) | river landscapes with cattle, Dordrecht | **Golden light.** Countryside "drenched in an atmospheric golden light", hilly ground in "the soft golden glow of early morning or evening", cattle silhouetted against a golden sky [V: NGA]. The model for the Delectable Mountains and Beulah. |
| **Philips Koninck** (1619-1688) | panoramic landscapes | **The panorama**: a high viewpoint over a flat land that runs in pale bands to an almost ruler-straight far horizon, the upper half all sky, cloud shadows crossing [M]. A level-wide establishing look; the view from Mount Clear. |
| **Hercules Segers** (c.1589-c.1638) | mountain etchings | **Strange imagined mountains**, valleys enclosed by rock, printed in coloured ink on **tinted grounds** (blue on greyish-blue, blue on cream with brown and purplish-white paint), each impression unique [V: Rijksmuseum]; 24 of his 53 etchings are mountain landscapes [V]. A painter who made the land itself uncanny without a single monster: the Valley of Humiliation and the Valley of the Shadow. |
| **Rembrandt** (1606-1669) | *The Three Trees* (etching, 1643); landscape drawings | Storm on the left, light on the right, three trees on a dike against a breaking sky, tiny lovers hidden in a bush, a fisherman, a cart: **a whole weather in one plate, and the small people only found on looking.** |
| **Vermeer** (1632-1675) | *The Little Street*, the *Milkmaid* | Already used in the game (room 2). Plain brick, whitewash, a woman at work in a doorway: the dignity of a poor house. |
| **Claude Lorrain** (1600-1682), **Nicolas Poussin** (1594-1665) | ideal landscapes; Claude's *Liber Veritatis* drawings | Claude: the sun in or near the middle of the picture, dark framing trees left and right (repoussoir), the eye drawn back through gold haze to the light. Poussin: ordered, grave, the landscape as moral argument. Both were collected in England by the end of the century [M]. |

**The aesthetic principles these share, put plainly:**
1. **Low horizon, big sky.** A third or less is land; the sky does the feeling.
2. **Bands, not objects.** Flat land is shown as overlapping strips of lighter and darker, each nearly one tone, with wet air between (the game's own rule).
3. **One key.** In the tonal phase the colour of the whole picture is decided first; everything sits inside it. This gives the owner a tool: each level's *key* (van Goyen brown for the Slough, Cuyp gold for the Mountains, Segers' blue-grey for the Valley).
4. **Light moves across the land.** Ruisdael's Haarlem views are lit by sun breaking through clouds in patches: one band lit, the next in shadow. A *moving* cloud shadow is an animatable version of the owner's "light on the ground is flat".
5. **People are small.** Tiny figures on the road (Ruisdael's *Wheat Fields*, Hobbema's man in the avenue, Koninck's specks): the land's size is shown by not shrinking the land.
6. **The moral landscape.** Dead tree, ruin, rainbow, light on a far church: the Dutch painters were reading land as meaning, just as Bunyan was writing it.

## 6. English art of his time

- **Wenceslaus Hollar** (1607-1677), the Bohemian etcher who drew England for 40 years. His **prospects**: the *Long View of London from Bankside* (1647, seven sheets) [V], *Greenwich* (1637, two plates) [V], Windsor and the four 1644 views (London, White Hall, Tothill Fields, Windsor) [V], and London before and after the Fire [V]. A Hollar prospect is a **long horizontal strip**: river or field in front, the town a band of roofs and towers along a ridge, steeples breaking the line, a high empty sky with a scroll of title; drawn in fine even line, almost no tone. It is how a 17th-century Englishman pictured a town: from outside, side-on, at a distance, as one silhouette. **Exactly Level 1's far town and Level 3's village of Morality.** Hollar also etched women's dress of every rank (*Theatrum Mulierum*, *Ornatus Muliebris Anglicanus*, 1640) [M]: the best source for what Christiana and the women of the later levels would wear.
- **Francis Barlow** (c.1626-1704), "the father of British sporting art": the illustrated *Aesop* (1666, 1687), birds, hounds, countryside, all as etchings [M]. English animals in English fields, drawn plainly: the source for any bird, sheep, dog or ox in the game.
- **Peter Lely** (1618-1680), court painter: silk, satin, bare shoulders, ringlets, a sleepy glamour [M]. The world Bunyan's book stands against. **Use Lely only for Vanity Fair** (and for Madam Wanton, Lord Hate-good, By-ends' kin).
- **Robert White**, the engraver of Bunyan's own portrait and the "sleeping portrait" frontispiece (1679) [V in report 1].
- **John Ogilby, *Britannia* (1675)**: see section 7.

## 7. Ogilby's strip maps: the 1675 side-scroller

*Britannia, Volume the First* (1675) [V]: the first road atlas of Britain, 100 plates of strip maps at one inch to the mile [V], about 7,500 miles of road surveyed with a measuring wheel [V]. Each plate holds six or seven vertical **scroll strips** about 2.5 inches wide; the top of one strip continues at the foot of the next [V]; the maps look like unrolled scrolls, "as if they were to be opened and used on the journey itself" [V]. Along the road, drawn small and side-on [M]: churches with spires, windmills, bridges, woods, hills shown as little profiles with "ascent" and "descent", inns, gallows, the names of villages, and the **turnings off the road** lettered with where they lead ("to Bedford", "to Ampthill"). A compass rose on each strip turns as the road turns. Each plate has a decorative cartouche with figures.

Why it matters: Ogilby's surveyors were walking England's roads with a wheel in the very years (1669 to 1675) Bunyan was in prison writing a road. **The 17th century already pictured a journey as one long strip, the road straight, the world drawn as landmarks beside it, with the wrong turnings marked and named.** Good-will's warning, "there are many ways butt down upon this, and they are crooked and wide" ({67}), is an Ogilby plate in words.

## 8. How this could shape the game (general)

1. **The contents page as an Ogilby plate.** Inside "THE CONTENTS." the levels become one road in scroll strips, each strip a level, drawn in Ogilby's engraved manner (fine black line on the laid paper, little side-on landmarks: a burning town, a slough, a hill, a gate, a house, a cross ...), the strip of the level the child has reached inked, those still ahead faint. Wrong turnings marked as Ogilby marks them: "to Morality", "to By-path Meadow". It joins report 1's "road maps" idea to its truer, earlier source. Free, in code.
2. **Emblem cards between levels.** A round or oval emblem frame on the cream page: a motto above (a line of Bunyan's or his margin text), one object drawn in a single tone (the burden, the roll, the key called Promise, the glass), a two-line rhyme below in Bunyan's own *Book for Boys and Girls* manner. The **"Comparison"** could be the child's touch: they touch the picture, and the second couplet (what it means) appears. Bunyan's own verses that close each stage of the book (e.g. at {46}, {60}, which the game has left out) are already emblem subscriptiones.
3. **A palette in "keys".** Give each level one painter's key, decided first, everything tinted into it: van Goyen's brown-green-grey; Cuyp's gold; Segers' blue on cream; Rembrandt's warm dark; Hollar's grey line on white. The game's rule "each layer one tone" becomes "each level one key".
4. **Moving cloud shadows.** A slow band of shadow crossing the flat land and a band of sun after it (Ruisdael's Haarlem), drawn flat as the owner requires. It makes a still layered scene breathe without anything "moving on its own" that a child must watch.
5. **The prospect for every town.** A town is always seen as a Hollar prospect: a band of roofs and spires on a ridge, side-on, far, in one tone. Vanity Fair, the City of Destruction, Morality, and the Celestial City seen from far (Hollar's London becomes the city's ghost, transfigured).
6. **Woodcut for the dark.** Where a thing must be shown but not dwelt on (Apollyon, the giant, the fiends), show it as a **ballad woodcut**: a small black-line picture on the page, framed, like a stock cut, not a full-screen creature. It follows the critics' rule "the place frightens and the creature is hardly seen", and it is what the 1680 woodcuts did [V, report 1].
7. **Real Bedfordshire, quietly.** Timber-framed jettied houses with brick infill and thatch for Level 1 instead of generic stone; a post mill on the rise; Elstow's detached tower; the flat vale then the sandy ridge. A child will not know it, but it is true.

## 9. Ideas level by level

Levels are the plan's table in `CLAUDE.md` (16 or 17 levels). Built ones get small touches; later ones get a master and a key.

1. **City of Destruction.** Make the town Bedfordshire: timber frames, jetties, brick nogging, thatch and a few tile roofs, a market hall like Elstow's Moot Hall at the street's end (one lit doorway is enough), a church with a **detached tower**. The far town seen from the wheat field as a **Hollar prospect** (one band of roofs and spires). Bills-of-mortality red crosses on a few doors is too dark for children; but a far orange glow on low cloud over the town at the start, as Londoners saw it in 1666, reads "destruction" without any figure. Key: van Goyen grey-brown in the rain to Ruisdael's sunlit wheat at the end.
2. **Slough of Despond.** Already Ansel Adams; its true 17th-century cousin is **van Goyen's tonal river views**: nearly one colour, low banks, wide sky, a ferry or a post on the water. A **Ruisdael rainbow** after Help draws him out would be the century's own sign of hope after the storm (as in *The Jewish Cemetery*): faint, once. Departure; owner's yes.
3. **Worldly Wiseman and the hill.** The village of Morality as a Hollar prospect (it already peeks out). Rembrandt's *Three Trees*: storm on one side, light on the other, as he turns aside. Beelzebub's castle: offer the owner **Elstow's detached bell tower** as the quieter, local, true model.
4. **The Wicket-Gate and the Interpreter's House.** The narrow way after **Hobbema's *Avenue***: one held still as Good-will says "Look before you": straight as a rule, poplars, a tiny Christian, low horizon (a framed picture, not a level, to keep the side view). The rooms are emblems: each room could open with its **motto** on the wall as a carved or painted text board (Puritan churches had such boards), e.g. "The things which are seen are temporal" in room 3.
5. **The Cross.** Already Friedrich. Emblem idea: the moment the burden is gone, a single **emblem card**: the burden in a round frame, motto "Blest Cross!", Bunyan's own lines. Koninck's high panorama for the wide look from the top.
6. **Hill Difficulty, the arbour, the lions.** The **Greensand Ridge road to Ampthill**: a sandy hollow way cut deep into a ridge, banks of sand and roots either side, the steepest climb in Bunyan's country. Simple and Sloth in fetters by the way could be a ballad-woodcut vignette. The lions chained in the dark: Segers-like rock, not big cats.
7. **Palace Beautiful.** **Houghton House** (a Jacobean brick house with stone loggias on the brow of the ridge, the tradition's model): drawn as a Hollar prospect at dusk, one window lit. Inside, the study with "records of the greatest antiquity" and the armoury: Dutch interiors (de Hooch's lit doorways through to a courtyard; Vermeer's light).
8. **Apollyon, the Valley of Humiliation.** Palmer is the Romantic answer; the 17th-century one is **Segers**: a closed valley of strange rock printed blue on a tinted ground. Apollyon himself as a woodcut-sized emblem shadow at most.
9. **Valley of the Shadow of Death.** Segers again, darker; Ruisdael's torrent and dead trees from *The Jewish Cemetery*; the path narrow between a ditch and a quag. Key: blue-black on cream, Segers' light-ink-on-dark-ground plate (the "Mountain Landscape with a Crest, second version" [V]) as the inverted look: pale lines on darkness.
10. **Faithful and Talkative.** A Koninck plain: two small men walking a flat land in bands under a huge sky. Gentle, long, the talk carried by the walk.
11. **Vanity Fair.** **Lely and the court** (silk, ringlets, gold), and the London of Hollar's *Long View* crowded with booths; the real model is **Stourbridge Fair** near Cambridge, the greatest fair in England in Bunyan's day [M]. Ballad woodcuts for the stalls' signs. Faithful's end: one woodcut emblem, the chariot above the fire (as the 1680s cuts did [V, report 1]), never shown full size.
12. **Hopeful, By-ends, Demas.** By-ends' party in Lely satin on the road; Demas's silver mine as a Segers hill with a glint; Lot's wife a pale pillar like a stone in a Ruisdael ruin.
13. **By-path Meadow and Doubting Castle.** By-path Meadow is a hedged **close** over a stile beside the road (the 17th-century field system exactly); the flood at night; the castle a plain square keep like a Bedfordshire church tower without its church. The **key called Promise** as an emblem card (key in a round frame, motto).
14. **The Delectable Mountains.** **Cuyp's gold**: shepherds, sheep and cattle on soft hills in evening light, silhouetted. The shepherds' glass ("perspective glass") through which the gate of the City is seen: the century of the telescope; show the City in a round lens (an emblem frame and a telescope view at once; Quarles has the Soul at a telescope [M]).
15. **Ignorance, the Flatterer, Atheist.** The Flatterer's net as a ballad woodcut; Ignorance's lane "from the country of Conceit" joining by a crooked way (Ogilby turning, labelled).
16. **The Enchanted Ground.** Van Goyen's tonal phase at its most drowsy: warm grey haze, a low sun, nothing sharp; and the danger is that it is beautiful.
17. **Beulah, the River, the Celestial City.** Cuyp's gold to Claude's sun in the middle of the picture, the City a **Hollar prospect made of light** (the same horizontal band of towers and spires the child saw burning in Level 1, now golden). That rhyme, the city seen first as destruction and last as glory, in the same 17th-century form, would close the game.
